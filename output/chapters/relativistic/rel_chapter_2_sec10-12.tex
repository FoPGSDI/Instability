\section{The analysis into normal modes: relativistic}
\label{sec:rel-10}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Covariant perturbation ansatz}
\label{sec:rel-10a}

In the classical theory (\S~10 of Chapter~II) every perturbed quantity is
expanded with the factor $\exp[i(k_x x + k_y y) + pt]$, where $p$ is an
unrestricted complex growth rate and $k = (k_x^2+k_y^2)^{1/2}$ is the
horizontal wave number.  The Newtonian dispersion relation that follows from
the linearised Boussinesq equations is a cubic polynomial in $p$, and all
three roots are physical.

In the BDNK (Bemfica--Disconzi--Noronha--Kovtun) relativistic formulation,
the perturbation equations are derived from first-order causal viscous
hydrodynamics.  Unlike the Israel--Stewart approach, which introduces
auxiliary relaxation equations (and consequently additional dynamical degrees
of freedom), BDNK achieves causality through the choice of hydrodynamic
frame and constrained first-order transport coefficients.  A single
essential feature distinguishes the BDNK perturbation analysis from the
classical one:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Causal propagation.}  The BDNK constitutive relations ensure
  that all signal speeds emerging from the dispersion relation are bounded
  by~$c$.  This is achieved not by adding relaxation equations, but by the
  algebraic structure of the first-order dissipative terms in a general
  hydrodynamic frame.  No auxiliary dynamical degrees of freedom are
  introduced.
\end{enumerate}

We work in the local rest frame of the static equilibrium and retain factors
of $c$ explicitly throughout, so that the non-relativistic limit $c\to\infty$
can be taken at every stage.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Perturbation ansatz.}
Ascribe to every linearised quantity (the vertical velocity perturbation~$W$,
the temperature perturbation~$\Theta$, and the vertical vorticity
perturbation~$Z$) a dependence on the horizontal coordinates and time of
the form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-88}
\exp\!\bigl[i(k_x\, x + k_y\, y) + \sigma t\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ may be complex and $k = (k_x^2+k_y^2)^{1/2}$.  The vertical
dependence remains a function to be determined: $W(z)$, $\Theta(z)$, etc.

Introducing the non-dimensional wave number $a = kd$ and the non-dimensional
growth rate $\sigma_* = \sigma d^2/\nu$ (as in the classical \S~10), together
with the operators
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-91}
D \equiv \frac{d}{dz}, \qquad
\mathcal{D}^2 \equiv D^2 - a^2,
\end{equation}
the linearised BDNK equations for the vertical modes reduce to the
following system.

\subsection{Relativistic normal-mode equations}
\label{sec:rel-10b}

In the BDNK formalism the dissipative contributions to the energy-momentum
tensor are first-order gradient terms: the shear stress is
$\Pi^{\mu\nu} = -2\shearvisc\, \sigma^{\mu\nu}$, the bulk viscous pressure
is $\Pi_{\mathrm{bulk}} = -\bulkvisc\, \nabla_\mu u^\mu$, and the heat flux
$Q^\mu$ includes both a Fourier-type term and frame-dependent corrections.
These enter the linearised conservation laws directly, without auxiliary
evolution equations.

The \emph{momentum equation} for the vertical velocity amplitude reads
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-92}
(\mathcal{D}^2 - \sigma_*)\,\mathcal{D}^2 W
= -\Rarel\, a^2 \Theta,
\end{equation}
where $\Rarel$ is the relativistic Rayleigh number
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-Rayleigh}
\Rarel = \frac{g\alpha\beta\, d^4}{\kappa\nu}
  \left[1 + \order{\frac{v^2}{c^2}}\right].
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:rel-2-92} is exactly Chandrasekhar's classical
equation~(92).  In BDNK there is no relaxation-time correction to this
equation; the dissipative terms (shear viscosity) enter at the same order
as in the classical Navier--Stokes theory.

The \emph{energy equation} for the temperature amplitude becomes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-93}
(\mathcal{D}^2 - \mathrm{p}\,\sigma_*)\,\Theta
= -\frac{\beta d^2}{\kappa}\, W,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{p} = \nu/\kappa$ is the Prandtl number.  Again, this is
identical to Chandrasekhar's equation~(93).  The BDNK heat flux is a
first-order gradient expression; its effect on the linearised energy
equation at the hydrodynamic level is captured entirely by the thermal
diffusivity~$\kappa$ (with $\order{v^2/c^2}$ corrections absorbed into the
definition of $\Rarel$).

The \emph{vorticity equation} is likewise unchanged:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-94}
(\mathcal{D}^2 - \sigma_*)\, Z = 0.
\end{equation}
The boundary conditions remain unchanged from the classical case:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-95}
\Theta = 0, \quad W = 0 \qquad\text{for } z = 0 \text{ and } 1,
\end{equation}
together with either $DW = 0$ (rigid) or $D^2 W = 0$ (free) on each
bounding surface.

\subsection{Dispersion relation and mode identification}
\label{sec:rel-10c}

Eliminating $\Theta$ between~\eqref{eq:rel-2-92} and~\eqref{eq:rel-2-93}
and seeking solutions proportional to $\sin(n\pi z)$ (the ``both free''
boundary prototype), one obtains the dispersion relation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-dispersion-benard}
\mathcal{P}(\sigma_*;\, a^2,\, \Rarel) = 0,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}$ is a polynomial of degree \emph{three} in~$\sigma_*$
--- the same order as in the classical theory.

\begin{relcorrection}
\textbf{BDNK vs.\ Israel--Stewart mode structure.}
In the Israel--Stewart formulation the dispersion relation is a fifth-order
polynomial in~$\sigma_*$ (cubic classical part plus two additional roots from
the relaxation equations for heat flux and shear stress).  The two extra
roots represent transient relaxation modes that decay on microscopic
time-scales and do not participate in convective onset.

In the BDNK formalism no auxiliary relaxation equations are introduced, so
the dispersion relation is \emph{third-order} --- precisely the same as in
the classical theory.  All three roots are physical (hydrodynamic) modes,
continuously connected to their Newtonian counterparts.  There are no
``spurious'' transient modes to discuss or discard.

The three physical modes are identical in both formulations: the BDNK and
Israel--Stewart theories agree on the hydrodynamic sector of the dispersion
relation.  The difference lies solely in the transient mode structure, which
is absent in BDNK.
\end{relcorrection}

\subsection{Causality constraint on group velocities}
\label{sec:rel-10d}

\begin{causalitycheck}
For any mode with dispersion relation $\sigma_*(a)$, define the group
velocity
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-vg}
v_g = \left|\frac{\partial\,\operatorname{Re}(\sigma_*)}
            {\partial a}\right| \frac{\nu}{d}.
\end{equation}
In the BDNK theory, causality ($v_g \le c$) is guaranteed by the algebraic
constraints on the transport coefficients and frame parameters:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-causality-bdnk}
\shearvisc > 0, \qquad
\bulkvisc + \tfrac{2}{3}\shearvisc > 0, \qquad
\text{plus coupled frame-coefficient inequalities},
\end{equation}
which ensure strong hyperbolicity of the full nonlinear BDNK system.

For the three physical (hydrodynamic) modes, which govern convective
onset, the group velocities are $\order{v}$ with $v \ll c$; causality
is satisfied trivially.  Unlike the Israel--Stewart theory, there are no
separate transient modes whose group velocities require an independent
causality check.
\end{causalitycheck}

\subsection{Horizontal velocity components}
\label{sec:rel-10e}

The reconstruction of the horizontal velocities $u$, $v$ from $W$ and $Z$ via
the stream-function decomposition (equations~(106)--(111) of \S~10a) is
purely kinematic and carries over unchanged to the relativistic case,
provided one works in the local rest frame.  No additional relativistic
corrections appear at this step.

\section{The principle of the exchange of stabilities: relativistic}
\label{sec:rel-11}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Statement of the problem}
\label{sec:rel-11a}

The classical proof (\S~11) demonstrates that for $R > 0$ the growth
rate $\sigma$ is purely real, so the transition from stability to instability
occurs through a \emph{stationary} (non-oscillatory) marginal state.  The key
question in the relativistic extension is:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does $\sigma_*$ remain real at the marginal state in the BDNK theory?}
\end{quote}

We shall prove that the answer is \emph{yes} --- and in fact the principle
holds \emph{exactly} at the marginal state ($\sigma = 0$), not merely
approximately.

\subsection{Proof for the physical modes}
\label{sec:rel-11b}

Define, as in the classical analysis,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-112}
G = \mathcal{D}^2 W, \qquad
F = \mathcal{D}^2(\mathcal{D}^2 - \sigma_*) W
  = (\mathcal{D}^2 - \sigma_*) G.
\end{equation}
From the BDNK normal-mode equations~\eqref{eq:rel-2-92}
and~\eqref{eq:rel-2-93}, after eliminating $\Theta$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-115}
(\mathcal{D}^2 - \mathrm{p}\,\sigma_*) F
  = -\Rarel\, a^2 W.
\end{equation}
This is \emph{identical} to Chandrasekhar's classical equation~(115).
In the BDNK formulation there is no remainder term
$\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{IS}}$ involving relaxation times; the first-order
dissipative terms have already been absorbed into the effective transport
coefficients.

Multiply~\eqref{eq:rel-2-115} by $F^*$ and integrate over
$z \in [0,1]$.  Following Chandrasekhar's integration-by-parts strategy
exactly (boundary terms vanish by the same argument as in the classical
case), we arrive at
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-118}
\int_0^1\!\bigl\{|DF|^2 + a^2|F|^2
  + \mathrm{p}\,\sigma_*|F|^2\bigr\}\,dz
  = \Rarel\, a^2\!\int_0^1 W F^*\,dz.
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is treated identically to the classical
equations~(119)--(123), yielding
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-124}
\int_0^1\!\bigl\{|DF|^2 + a^2|F|^2
  + \mathrm{p}\,\sigma_*|F|^2\bigr\}\,dz
  = \Rarel\, a^2\!\int_0^1\!\bigl\{|G|^2
    + \sigma_*^*\bigl[|DW|^2 + a^2|W|^2\bigr]\bigr\}\,dz.
\end{equation}
Taking the imaginary part:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-125}
\operatorname{Im}(\sigma_*)
  \left\{\mathrm{p}\!\int_0^1|F|^2\,dz
    + \Rarel\, a^2\!\int_0^1
      \bigl[|DW|^2 + a^2|W|^2\bigr]\,dz\right\}
= 0.
\end{equation}
The factor in braces is positive definite for $\Rarel > 0$.
Therefore:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-126}
\operatorname{Im}(\sigma_*) = 0 \qquad\text{exactly}.
\end{equation}

\begin{relcorrection}
\textbf{Exchange of stabilities (BDNK).}
In the BDNK formalism, the linearised perturbation equations for the
hydrodynamic modes take exactly the same form as in the classical
Navier--Stokes theory (with $\order{v^2/c^2}$ corrections absorbed into
$\Rarel$).  Consequently, Chandrasekhar's classical proof of the exchange
of stabilities applies \emph{without modification}: the imaginary part
of $\sigma_*$ vanishes exactly for $\Rarel > 0$.

This is in contrast to the Israel--Stewart formulation, where the
relaxation terms introduce an $\order{v^2/c^2}$ imaginary contribution
to $\sigma_*$.  That contribution was always negligible in practice, but
in BDNK it is absent entirely.  The reason is clear: BDNK has no auxiliary
relaxation equations, so there are no additional terms in the dispersion
relation that could break the classical exchange-of-stabilities argument.

At the marginal state ($\sigma = 0$), the IS and BDNK theories give
\emph{identical} results, since the IS relaxation terms are all
proportional to $\sigma$ and vanish at $\sigma = 0$.  The difference
between the two formulations appears only away from the marginal state,
in the transient dynamics and mode structure at finite $\sigma$.
\end{relcorrection}

\section{The marginal state and the relativistic characteristic value
  problem}
\label{sec:rel-12}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Marginal-state equations}
\label{sec:rel-12a}

Since the exchange of stabilities holds exactly in BDNK
(see \S~\ref{sec:rel-11}), the marginal state is stationary:
$\sigma_* = 0$.  Setting $\sigma_* = 0$ in the normal-mode
equations~\eqref{eq:rel-2-92} and~\eqref{eq:rel-2-93}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-127}
(\mathcal{D}^2)^2 W
= (D^2-a^2)^2 W
= +\Rarel\, a^2 \Theta,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-128}
(D^2-a^2)\Theta = -\frac{\beta d^2}{\kappa}\, W.
\end{equation}

\begin{relcorrection}
\textbf{Key observation.}  At marginal stability ($\sigma_* = 0$), the
BDNK dissipative terms --- which in the linearised equations are
proportional to the time-derivative (i.e., to~$\sigma_*$) --- vanish
identically.  The marginal-state equations therefore take \emph{exactly
the same form} as the classical equations~(127)--(128) of Chandrasekhar,
with $R$ replaced by $\Rarel$.

This result is \emph{identical} to what one obtains in the Israel--Stewart
formulation.  In IS, the relaxation correction terms (proportional to
$\taupi$ and $\tauq$) all carry a factor of~$\sigma_*$ and likewise
vanish at $\sigma_* = 0$.  At the marginal state, the two formulations ---
BDNK and IS --- yield the same eigenvalue problem.  This is physically
necessary: the marginal state is a time-independent configuration, so
neither the hyperbolic relaxation structure (IS) nor the causal frame
corrections (BDNK) can influence the result.

The entire effect of relativistic corrections is thus absorbed into the
definition of $\Rarel$.
\end{relcorrection}

Eliminating $\Theta$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-129}
(D^2 - a^2)^3 W = -\Rarel\, a^2\, W,
\end{equation}
with boundary conditions
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-130}
W = 0, \quad (D^2-a^2)^2 W = 0 \qquad\text{for } z = 0 \text{ and } 1,
\end{equation}
and either $DW = 0$ (rigid) or $D^2 W = 0$ (free) on each surface.

Alternatively, eliminating $W$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-131}
(D^2-a^2)^3\Theta = -\Rarel\, a^2\, \Theta,
\end{equation}
with boundary conditions
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-2-132}
\Theta = 0, \quad (D^2-a^2)\Theta = 0 \qquad\text{for } z = 0 \text{ and } 1,
\end{equation}
and appropriate conditions on $D(D^2-a^2)\Theta$ depending on whether
each surface is rigid or free.

\subsection{The characteristic value problem for $\Rarel$}
\label{sec:rel-12b}

Equations~\eqref{eq:rel-2-129}--\eqref{eq:rel-2-130} (or equivalently
\eqref{eq:rel-2-131}--\eqref{eq:rel-2-132}) constitute a sixth-order
ordinary differential equation with six homogeneous boundary conditions.
A non-trivial solution exists only for particular values of $\Rarel$ at
given~$a^2$; we thus have a \emph{characteristic value problem} for the
relativistic Rayleigh number.

Let $\Rarel^{(c)}(a^2)$ denote the lowest eigenvalue for a given~$a^2$.
The critical relativistic Rayleigh number is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-Rac}
\Rarel^{(\mathrm{crit})}
= \min_{a^2} \Rarel^{(c)}(a^2).
\end{equation}

Since the eigenvalue problem at $\sigma_* = 0$ has exactly the same
structure as in the classical theory, we can write
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-Rac-expansion}
\Rarel^{(\mathrm{crit})}
= R_c^{(\mathrm{class})}
  \left[1 + \order{\frac{v^2}{c^2}}\right],
\end{equation}
where $R_c^{(\mathrm{class})}$ is the classical critical Rayleigh number
(e.g.\ $R_c = 27\pi^4/4$ for both surfaces free).  The relativistic
correction enters solely through the $\order{v^2/c^2}$ terms in the
definition of $\Rarel$ (equation~\eqref{eq:rel-Rayleigh}).

The critical wave number $a_c$ at which $\Rarel^{(\mathrm{crit})}$ is
attained is likewise corrected only at $\order{v^2/c^2}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-ac}
a_c^{(\mathrm{rel})}
= a_c^{(\mathrm{class})}
  \left[1 + \order{\frac{v^2}{c^2}}\right].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Comparison of BDNK and Israel--Stewart at and near marginal state}
\label{sec:rel-12c}

At marginal stability ($\sigma_* = 0$), the BDNK and Israel--Stewart
formulations yield \emph{identical} eigenvalue problems and hence identical
critical Rayleigh numbers.  This is because:
\begin{enumerate}
\item In IS, every relaxation correction term is proportional
  to~$\sigma_*$ and vanishes at $\sigma_* = 0$.
\item In BDNK, the dissipative terms (first-order gradients) likewise
  contribute only through time-derivative terms proportional to~$\sigma_*$
  in the linearised equations, and vanish at $\sigma_* = 0$.
\end{enumerate}
The two formulations differ only \emph{away from} the marginal state:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mode structure:} IS has a fifth-order dispersion relation
  (3 physical + 2 transient relaxation modes); BDNK has a third-order
  dispersion relation (3 physical modes only).
\item \textbf{Transient dynamics:} For $\sigma_* \ne 0$, IS dispersion
  receives corrections of order $\sigma_* \taupi\nu/d^2$ and
  $\sigma_* \tauq\kappa/d^2$ from the relaxation modes.  BDNK has no
  such corrections.
\item \textbf{Exchange of stabilities:} In BDNK,
  $\operatorname{Im}(\sigma_*) = 0$ exactly.  In IS,
  $\operatorname{Im}(\sigma_*) = \order{v^2/c^2}$ (negligible but
  non-zero).
\end{itemize}

\begin{causalitycheck}
\textbf{Summary of causality verification for the eigenvalue problem.}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The three physical modes satisfy
  $|v_g| \ll c$ for all wave numbers relevant to convective onset.

\item Causality is guaranteed by the BDNK strong hyperbolicity conditions
  on the transport coefficients and frame parameters.  No separate
  transient-mode causality check is required (there are no transient modes
  in BDNK).

\item The marginal-state eigenvalue problem ($\sigma_* = 0$) involves no
  time derivatives and is automatically causal.

\item The critical Rayleigh number and critical wave number receive only
  $\order{v^2/c^2}$ corrections relative to their classical values.
\end{enumerate}

All mode frequencies therefore give subluminal group velocities, and the
relativistic eigenvalue problem is well-posed.
\end{causalitycheck}

\subsection{Non-relativistic limit}
\label{sec:rel-12d}

Taking $c \to \infty$ (hence $\Rarel \to R$), the entire system of
equations in \S\S~\ref{sec:rel-10}--\ref{sec:rel-12} reduces to
Chandrasekhar's classical equations~(88)--(132) of Chapter~II,
\S\S~10--12.  The third-order BDNK dispersion relation reduces to the
classical cubic.  The exchange of stabilities is exact (as it is already
at finite~$c$ in BDNK), and the characteristic value problem for $R$
is recovered without modification.

\section{Astrophysical applications: BDNK vs.\ classical thermal mode structure}
\label{sec:rel-12-applications}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The distinction between the BDNK and Israel--Stewart dispersion
relations has concrete consequences for astrophysical fluids.

\subsection{Thermal diffusion in neutron star crusts}

At $\rho\sim 10^{13}\;\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $T\sim 10^{9}$\,K,
$\kappa_T\sim 10^{5}\;\mathrm{cm^{2}\,s^{-1}}$ and
$\mathrm{Pr}\sim 10^{-5}$.  For $\lambda=10$\,m:
$\omega_{\mathrm{heat}} \approx -3.6\times 10^{6}\;i\;\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
BDNK causality corrections become significant only at
$\lambda\lesssim 2\times 10^{-5}$\,cm.
In IS, the transient relaxation mode at $\omega\sim -i/\tau_q$
($\tau_q\sim 10^{-13}$\,s) is $10^{7}$ orders of magnitude faster and
physically irrelevant for convective stability.

\subsection{QGP thermal modes at RHIC/LHC}

At $T=300$\,MeV, $\tau_q\sim 0.1$\,fm/$c\approx 3\times 10^{-25}$\,s.
For a QGP fireball of $R\sim 5$\,fm, the IS transient mode frequency
exceeds the hydrodynamic frequency by a factor 10--100.  In BDNK
these spurious modes do not exist.
