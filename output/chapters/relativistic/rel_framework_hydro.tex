\chapter{Relativistic Hydrodynamics Framework}
\label{ch:rel-framework}

In Chandrasekhar's original treatment, all fluid motions are assumed
non-relativistic: the fluid speed $v$ satisfies $v \ll c$, and the
pressure contribution to inertia is negligible ($p \ll \varepsilon$,
where $\varepsilon$ is the energy density including rest mass).  When
either of these conditions is relaxed---as in neutron star interiors,
accretion flows near compact objects, or the early universe---special
relativistic hydrodynamics must replace the classical Euler and
Navier--Stokes equations.  This chapter develops the relativistic
framework that underlies every subsequent modification of the
stability analyses.

\section{Special Relativistic Perfect-Fluid Hydrodynamics}
\label{sec:sr-hydro}
%% ====================================================================

\subsection{Four-velocity and thermodynamic quantities}
\label{sec:four-vel}

Let $x^{\mu} = (ct, x^i)$ denote Minkowski coordinates with the
metric
\begin{equation}
  \eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1).
  \label{eq:rel-metric}
\end{equation}
The fluid four-velocity is
\begin{equation}
  u^{\mu} = \Lf\,(c, v^i),
  \qquad
  \Lf = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}},
  \label{eq:rel-four-vel}
\end{equation}
satisfying the normalization
\begin{equation}
  u^{\mu}\, u_{\mu} = -c^2.
  \label{eq:rel-norm}
\end{equation}
The projection tensor orthogonal to $u^{\mu}$ is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu} + \frac{u^{\mu}\, u^{\nu}}{c^2},
  \label{eq:rel-proj}
\end{equation}
which satisfies $\Delta^{\mu\nu} u_{\nu} = 0$ and
$\Delta^{\mu}{}_{\alpha}\,\Delta^{\alpha\nu} = \Delta^{\mu\nu}$.

In the local rest frame of the fluid, the thermodynamic state is
specified by the energy density $\varepsilon$ (including rest-mass
energy $\rho_0 c^2$), the isotropic pressure $p$, the temperature
$T$, and the baryon number density $n$.  The relativistic enthalpy
density is
\begin{equation}
  \enthalpy = \frac{\varepsilon + p}{c^2},
  \label{eq:rel-enthalpy}
\end{equation}
which plays the role of an effective inertial mass density.  In the
non-relativistic limit, $\enthalpy \to \rho_0$ since $p \ll \rho_0 c^2$.

\subsection{Energy-momentum tensor for a perfect fluid}
\label{sec:emt-perfect}

The energy-momentum tensor for a perfect relativistic fluid is
\begin{equation}
  T^{\mu\nu} = \frac{(\varepsilon + p)}{c^2}\, u^{\mu}\, u^{\nu}
    + p\, \eta^{\mu\nu}
    = \enthalpy\, u^{\mu}\, u^{\nu} + p\, \eta^{\mu\nu}.
  \label{eq:rel-emt-perfect}
\end{equation}
This is the unique symmetric tensor that (i) reduces to
$\mathrm{diag}(\varepsilon, p, p, p)$ in the rest frame and
(ii) transforms covariantly under Lorentz boosts.

\subsection{Conservation laws}
\label{sec:conservation}

Two independent conservation laws govern perfect-fluid dynamics:

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{(a) Baryon number conservation.}  With $\rho_0$ the
rest-mass density (or equivalently $n$ the baryon number density),
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_{\mu}(\rho_0\, u^{\mu}) = 0
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \partial_{\mu}(\rho_0\, u^{\mu}) = 0.
  \label{eq:rel-continuity}
\end{equation}
In Minkowski space, $\nabla_{\mu} = \partial_{\mu}$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{(b) Energy-momentum conservation.}
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{\mu} T^{\mu\nu} = 0.
  \label{eq:rel-emt-cons}
\end{equation}
This single tensorial equation contains both the energy equation
and the three momentum equations.

\subsection{3+1 decomposition: relativistic Euler equations}
\label{sec:3plus1}

We decompose \eqref{eq:rel-emt-cons} by projecting along and
orthogonal to $u^{\mu}$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Energy equation} ($u_{\nu}\,\partial_{\mu} T^{\mu\nu} = 0$):
\begin{equation}
  u^{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu}\varepsilon
  + (\varepsilon + p)\,\partial_{\mu} u^{\mu} = 0.
  \label{eq:rel-energy}
\end{equation}
This states that the rate of change of energy density in the comoving
frame is balanced by $p\,dV$ work.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Momentum equation}
($\Delta^{\nu}{}_{\alpha}\,\partial_{\mu} T^{\mu\alpha} = 0$):
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\varepsilon + p}{c^2}\, u^{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu} u^{\nu}
  + \Delta^{\nu\alpha}\,\partial_{\alpha} p = 0.
  \label{eq:rel-momentum}
\end{equation}
The pre-factor $(\varepsilon + p)/c^2 = \enthalpy$ shows that
\emph{pressure contributes to inertia} in relativity---the single
most important qualitative difference from Newtonian fluid dynamics.

Writing $u^{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu} = \Lf\,(\partial_t/c + v^i\,\partial_i)$
and expanding, the spatial components of
\eqref{eq:rel-momentum} become
\begin{equation}
  \enthalpy\,\Lf^2\!\left(\frac{\partial v^i}{\partial t}
    + v^j\,\partial_j v^i\right)
  = -\partial_i p
    - \frac{v^i}{c^2}\,\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}
    + O\!\left(\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)\text{corrections}.
  \label{eq:rel-euler-spatial}
\end{equation}
In the non-relativistic limit ($\Lf \to 1$, $\enthalpy \to \rho_0$,
$p/c^2 \to 0$), this reduces to Euler's equation
$\rho_0(\partial_t v^i + v^j\partial_j v^i) = -\partial_i p$.

\section{Dissipative Relativistic Hydrodynamics: BDNK Theory}
\label{sec:bdnk}
%% ====================================================================

\subsection{Failure of relativistic Navier--Stokes (Eckart theory)}
\label{sec:eckart-failure}

The na\"{\i}ve relativistic generalization of the Navier--Stokes
equations---due to Eckart (1940) and later Landau \&
Lifshitz---writes the dissipative fluxes as instantaneous,
algebraic functions of the thermodynamic gradients:
\begin{equation}
  \pi^{\mu\nu}_{\mathrm{Eckart}}
    = -2\,\shearvisc\,\sigma^{\mu\nu},
  \quad
  \Pi_{\mathrm{Eckart}} = -\bulkvisc\,\nabla_{\alpha} u^{\alpha},
  \quad
  q^{\mu}_{\mathrm{Eckart}}
    = -\kappa\,\Delta^{\mu\nu}\!\left(\partial_{\nu} T
      + T\,\frac{u^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha} u_{\nu}}{c^2}\right),
  \label{eq:eckart-constitutive}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma^{\mu\nu}$ is the shear tensor
\begin{equation}
  \sigma^{\mu\nu} = \Delta^{\mu\alpha}\Delta^{\nu\beta}
    \!\left(\frac{\partial_{\alpha} u_{\beta}
      + \partial_{\beta} u_{\alpha}}{2}\right)
    - \frac{1}{3}\,\Delta^{\mu\nu}\,\nabla_{\alpha} u^{\alpha}.
  \label{eq:shear-tensor}
\end{equation}

The fundamental problem is that equations
\eqref{eq:eckart-constitutive}, when substituted into
$\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, yield \emph{parabolic} partial
differential equations.  Parabolic equations have infinite signal
propagation speed: a localized temperature perturbation is felt
\emph{instantaneously} everywhere, violating relativistic causality
($v_{\mathrm{signal}} \le c$).

Moreover, the equilibrium state is \emph{unstable} in the
Eckart theory: small perturbations about a uniform state grow
exponentially in time, as shown by Hiscock \& Lindblom (1985).
Both pathologies---acausality and instability---are fatal.

The root cause is not the first-order gradient expansion itself,
but the restrictive frame choice (Landau or Eckart) that sets
certain out-of-equilibrium contributions to zero.  As shown by
Bemfica, Disconzi \& Noronha (2018) and Kovtun (2019), a more
general hydrodynamic frame restores both causality and stability
\emph{at first order in gradients}.

\subsection{BDNK general hydrodynamic frame}
\label{sec:bdnk-frame}

The Bemfica--Disconzi--Noronha--Kovtun (BDNK) formalism
(Bemfica et~al.\ 2018, 2019; Kovtun 2019; Hoult \& Kovtun 2020)
resolves the acausality and instability problems of Eckart's and
Landau's theories by working in a \emph{general hydrodynamic frame},
in which the fluid four-velocity $u^{\mu}$ is defined by neither the
Landau condition ($q^{\mu} = 0$) nor the Eckart condition
($u^{\mu} \propto n^{\mu}$, the particle current).

In a general frame, the out-of-equilibrium energy-momentum tensor is
decomposed as
\begin{equation}
  T^{\mu\nu} = E\,\frac{u^{\mu}\,u^{\nu}}{c^{2}}
    + P\,\Delta^{\mu\nu}
    + \frac{Q^{\mu}\,u^{\nu} + Q^{\nu}\,u^{\mu}}{c^{2}}
    + \Pi^{\mu\nu},
  \label{eq:bdnk-emt-decomp}
\end{equation}
where the out-of-equilibrium scalars $E$, $P$, the heat flux
$Q^{\mu}$, and the anisotropic stress $\Pi^{\mu\nu}$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
  Q^{\mu}\, u_{\mu} = 0,
  \qquad
  \Pi^{\mu\nu}\, u_{\nu} = 0,
  \qquad
  \Pi^{\mu}{}_{\mu} = 0,
  \qquad
  \Pi^{\mu\nu} = \Pi^{\nu\mu}.
  \label{eq:bdnk-constraints}
\end{equation}
The key distinction from the Landau frame is that $E \neq \varepsilon$,
$P \neq p$, and $Q^{\mu} \neq 0$ out of equilibrium.  The difference
between $E$ and $\varepsilon$ (and between $P$ and $p$) is of
first order in gradients and is parametrized by the \emph{frame
coefficients}.

\subsection{BDNK first-order constitutive relations}
\label{sec:bdnk-constitutive}

The BDNK constitutive relations express the dissipative corrections
as first-order gradient terms with additional frame-dependent pieces.
Denoting the expansion scalar $\theta = \nabla_{\mu}u^{\mu}$, the
BDNK relations are:

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Out-of-equilibrium energy density:}
\begin{equation}
  E = \varepsilon + \varepsilon_{1},
  \qquad
  \varepsilon_{1}
    = -\zeta_{1}\,\theta
      - \beta_{1}\,\frac{u^{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu} T}{T}
      - \alpha_{1}\,u^{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu}\!\left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right),
  \label{eq:bdnk-energy-corr}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta_1$, $\beta_1$, and $\alpha_1$ are frame coefficients
(transport parameters with no analogue in classical fluid mechanics)
that vanish in the Landau frame.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Bulk viscous pressure:}
\begin{equation}
  P = p + \Pi_{\mathrm{bulk}},
  \qquad
  \Pi_{\mathrm{bulk}} = -\bulkvisc\,\theta
    + \text{(frame-dependent gradient terms)},
  \label{eq:bdnk-bulk}
\end{equation}
where $\bulkvisc$ is the bulk viscosity.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Anisotropic (shear) stress:}
\begin{equation}
  \Pi^{\mu\nu} = -2\,\shearvisc\,\sigma^{\mu\nu}.
  \label{eq:bdnk-shear}
\end{equation}
This has the same form as in Navier--Stokes theory; the shear sector
requires no relaxation equation because the frame choice already
ensures causal propagation.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Heat flux:}
\begin{equation}
  Q^{\mu} = -\kappa\, T\,\Delta^{\mu\alpha}\,
    \partial_{\alpha}\!\left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right)
    + \text{frame correction terms},
  \label{eq:bdnk-heat}
\end{equation}
where the frame correction terms are first-order gradient
contributions proportional to $\Delta^{\mu\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}T$,
$\Delta^{\mu\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}p$, and so on, with coefficients
determined by the general-frame parameters.

\medskip
The crucial point is that these are \emph{algebraic} constitutive
relations (no time derivatives of the dissipative fluxes appear), yet
the resulting system is causal.  The dissipative fluxes are not
independent dynamical variables; they are determined instantaneously
by the gradients of $(\varepsilon, n, u^{\mu})$.  This is in stark
contrast to the Israel--Stewart approach, which promotes
$\pi^{\mu\nu}$, $q^{\mu}$, and $\Pi$ to independent degrees of
freedom obeying relaxation equations.

\subsection{Causality and strong hyperbolicity}
\label{sec:bdnk-causality}

The central result of the BDNK formalism is that the first-order
equations of motion---obtained by substituting
\eqref{eq:bdnk-energy-corr}--\eqref{eq:bdnk-heat} into the
conservation law $\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu} = 0$---form a
\textbf{strongly hyperbolic} system of partial differential equations,
provided the transport coefficients and frame coefficients satisfy
certain coupled inequalities.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Strong hyperbolicity conditions.}
The BDNK system is strongly hyperbolic (and hence causal, with all
characteristic speeds bounded by~$c$) if and only if the following
conditions are satisfied:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Positive shear viscosity:} $\shearvisc > 0$.
\item \textbf{Positive bulk--shear combination:}
  $\bulkvisc + \tfrac{2}{3}\,\shearvisc > 0$.
\item \textbf{Frame coefficient inequalities:}
  The frame parameters $(\varepsilon_1, \beta_1, \alpha_1)$ and
  the transport coefficients $(\shearvisc, \bulkvisc, \kappa)$
  must satisfy a set of coupled nonlinear inequalities that
  ensure all characteristic velocities of the system remain
  subluminal.  These inequalities replace the Israel--Stewart
  requirement that all relaxation times be positive
  ($\tau_{\pi} > 0$, $\tau_{q} > 0$, $\tau_{\Pi} > 0$).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Contrast with Israel--Stewart.}
The Israel--Stewart system is only \emph{approximately} hyperbolic:
causality has been established at the linearized level around
equilibrium, but a rigorous proof of strong hyperbolicity for the
full nonlinear system is lacking.  In contrast, BDNK strong
hyperbolicity has been proven rigorously for the full nonlinear
equations by Bemfica, Disconzi \& Noronha (2018) and extended to
include charge diffusion by Bemfica et~al.\ (2023).  Strong
hyperbolicity guarantees:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Well-posedness} of the initial-value problem,
\item \emph{Finite propagation speeds} for all perturbations,
\item \emph{Stability} of the equilibrium state, and
\item \emph{Continuous dependence} on initial data---essential for
  predictive stability analyses.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Heat conduction in the BDNK framework}
\label{sec:bdnk-heat-conduction}

To illustrate how BDNK achieves causality without relaxation
equations, consider heat conduction in one spatial dimension.  In the
Eckart (Landau-frame) theory, Fourier's law $q = -\kappa\,\partial_x T$
combined with the energy equation yields the parabolic heat equation
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
    = \frac{\kappa}{c_v}\,\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}
  \qquad\text{(parabolic, acausal)},
  \label{eq:heat-parabolic}
\end{equation}
where $c_v = \partial\varepsilon/\partial T$.  This equation has
infinite signal propagation speed.

In the BDNK framework, the constitutive relations
\eqref{eq:bdnk-energy-corr}--\eqref{eq:bdnk-heat} include
frame-dependent gradient terms in the energy density and heat flux.
These additional terms modify the principal part of the differential
operator, converting the system from parabolic to hyperbolic.  The
resulting heat equation remains \emph{first-order in time}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
    = \kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}
    + \text{(higher-gradient corrections from frame terms)},
  \label{eq:bdnk-heat-firstorder}
\end{equation}
but the dispersion relation at high wave numbers is modified by the
frame coefficients so that all modes propagate causally.
Specifically, for a plane wave $\propto e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$, the
BDNK dispersion relation yields $|\mathrm{Re}(\omega)/k| \le c$
for all $k$, without the need for a second time derivative (telegraph
equation) as in Israel--Stewart.

This is the essential conceptual difference: Israel--Stewart restores
causality by introducing a second time derivative (making the heat
equation hyperbolic via a ``telegraph'' term $\tau_{q}\,\partial^{2}_{t}T$),
whereas BDNK restores causality through the frame-dependent gradient
terms in the constitutive relations, which modify the spatial
principal symbol of the system.

\section{Linearized Perturbation Theory}
\label{sec:linearized}
%% ====================================================================

\subsection{Background state}
\label{sec:background}

We consider small perturbations about a static, uniform equilibrium
state characterized by
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = \varepsilon_0,
  \quad
  p = p_0,
  \quad
  \rho_0 = \text{const},
  \quad
  u^{\mu} = (c, 0, 0, 0),
  \quad
  T = T_0.
  \label{eq:background}
\end{equation}
All dissipative fluxes vanish in equilibrium: the BDNK constitutive
corrections $\varepsilon_1$, $\Pi_{\mathrm{bulk}}$, $Q^{\mu}$, and
$\Pi^{\mu\nu}$ are identically zero when all gradients vanish.

\subsection{Perturbation ansatz}
\label{sec:perturbation-ansatz}

Write
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = \varepsilon_0 + \delta\varepsilon,
  \quad
  p = p_0 + \delta p,
  \quad
  u^{\mu} = (c, \delta v^i),
  \label{eq:perturbations}
\end{equation}
where $\delta\varepsilon$, $\delta p$, $\delta v^i$ are first-order
quantities.  The four-velocity normalization
\eqref{eq:rel-norm} is automatically satisfied to first order since
$u_{\mu}\,u^{\mu} = -c^2 + |\delta v|^2 = -c^2 + O(2)$.

\subsection{Linearized equations for a perfect fluid}
\label{sec:linearized-eqs}

Linearizing the continuity equation \eqref{eq:rel-continuity}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\,\delta\rho_0}{\partial t}
    + \rho_0\,\partial_i\,\delta v^i = 0.
  \label{eq:lin-continuity}
\end{equation}

Linearizing the energy equation \eqref{eq:rel-energy}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\,\delta\varepsilon}{\partial t}
    + (\varepsilon_0 + p_0)\,\partial_i\,\delta v^i = 0.
  \label{eq:lin-energy}
\end{equation}

Linearizing the momentum equation \eqref{eq:rel-momentum}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\varepsilon_0 + p_0}{c^2}\,
    \frac{\partial\,\delta v^i}{\partial t}
  = -\partial_i\,\delta p.
  \label{eq:lin-momentum}
\end{equation}

Note the factor $(\varepsilon_0 + p_0)/c^2$ in the momentum
equation: pressure contributes to the effective inertia.

\subsection{Sound waves and dispersion relation}
\label{sec:sound-waves}

For an isentropic process, $\delta p = \cs^2\,\delta\varepsilon$
where
\begin{equation}
  \cs^2 = \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\varepsilon}\right)_{\!s}
  \label{eq:sound-speed-def}
\end{equation}
is the relativistic sound speed squared.  Seeking plane-wave
solutions $\propto \exp[i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x} - \omega t)]$
and combining \eqref{eq:lin-energy}--\eqref{eq:lin-momentum},
we obtain the dispersion relation
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\omega^2 = \cs^2\, k^2.}
  \label{eq:rel-dispersion}
\end{equation}
This is identical in form to the non-relativistic result, but the
sound speed is defined differently: through
$(\partial p/\partial\varepsilon)_s$ rather than
$(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s$.

\subsection{Causality bound on the sound speed}
\label{sec:causality-bound}

Since no signal may propagate faster than light,
\begin{equation}
  \cs^2 \le c^2
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\varepsilon}\right)_{\!s} \le 1
  \quad\text{(in units where $c = 1$)}.
  \label{eq:causality-cs}
\end{equation}
This places a non-trivial constraint on the equation of state.  For
an ideal gas with adiabatic index $\hat{\gamma}$, one can show
$\cs^2 = \hat{\gamma}\,p/(\varepsilon + p)$, which automatically
satisfies $\cs^2 < c^2$ for $\hat{\gamma} \le 2$.  For a radiation
fluid ($\varepsilon = 3p$), $\cs^2 = c^2/3$.

\subsection{Dissipative corrections to the dispersion relation}
\label{sec:diss-dispersion}

Including BDNK dissipation (Sec.~\ref{sec:bdnk}), the dispersion
relation for longitudinal modes becomes
\begin{equation}
  \omega^2
  + i\omega\,\frac{(\tfrac{4}{3}\shearvisc + \bulkvisc)}%
    {(\varepsilon_0 + p_0)/c^2}\,k^2
  - \cs^2\, k^2 = 0.
  \label{eq:bdnk-dispersion}
\end{equation}
This is a \emph{quadratic} polynomial in $\omega$---yielding exactly
two longitudinal sound modes (propagating with viscous damping)---in
contrast to the Israel--Stewart dispersion relation, which is a
higher-order polynomial admitting additional ``relaxation modes''
with no physical counterpart in the hydrodynamic regime.

At low frequencies ($\omega \ll c\,k$), equation
\eqref{eq:bdnk-dispersion} reduces to the standard viscous damping:
sound waves propagate at speed $\cs$ and are attenuated at the rate
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Im}(\omega)
    = -\frac{(\tfrac{4}{3}\shearvisc + \bulkvisc)}%
      {2\,(\varepsilon_0 + p_0)/c^2}\,k^2 + O(k^4).
  \label{eq:bdnk-damping-rate}
\end{equation}
Causality is ensured not by relaxation-time corrections at
$O(\tau\,\omega^2 k^2)$, but by the BDNK strong hyperbolicity
conditions (Sec.~\ref{sec:bdnk-causality}), which guarantee that all
characteristic speeds---including those at arbitrarily high $k$---remain bounded by~$c$.

The transverse (shear) sector likewise has only the physical
diffusive mode:
\begin{equation}
  \omega = -i\,\frac{\shearvisc}{(\varepsilon_0 + p_0)/c^2}\,k^2
    + O(k^4)
    \qquad\text{(shear mode)},
  \label{eq:bdnk-shear-mode}
\end{equation}
which is diffusive at low $k$ and becomes a propagating, causally
bounded mode at high $k$ due to the BDNK frame terms.

\section{Non-Relativistic Limit}
\label{sec:nr-limit}
%% ====================================================================

\subsection{Systematic expansion in $v/c$}
\label{sec:vc-expansion}

To recover Chandrasekhar's original equations, we take
$v/c \to 0$ systematically.  The relevant limits are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Lorentz factor:} $\Lf = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}v^2/c^2
  + O(v^4/c^4) \to 1$.

\item \textbf{Energy density:}
  $\varepsilon = \rho_0 c^2 + \rho_0 e + O(v^2/c^2)$,
  where $e$ is the specific internal energy, so
  $\varepsilon + p \approx \rho_0 c^2\bigl(1 + e/c^2 + p/(\rho_0 c^2)\bigr)$.

\item \textbf{Enthalpy density:}
  $\enthalpy = (\varepsilon + p)/c^2 \to \rho_0
  + O(p/c^2, e/c^2)$.

\item \textbf{Four-velocity:}
  $u^{\mu} = (c, v^i) + O(v^2/c)$.

\item \textbf{Sound speed:}
  $\cs^2 = (\partial p/\partial\varepsilon)_s
  = (\partial p/\partial\rho)_s / (1 + e/c^2 + p/(\rho c^2))
  \to (\partial p/\partial\rho)_s + O(c^{-2})$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Recovery of Euler and Navier--Stokes equations}
\label{sec:euler-recovery}

In the limit $v/c \to 0$:

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Continuity:}  Equation \eqref{eq:rel-continuity} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\rho_0}{\partial t}
    + \nabla\cdot(\rho_0\,\mathbf{v}) = 0.
  \label{eq:nr-continuity}
\end{equation}

\noindent
\textbf{Momentum:}  Equation \eqref{eq:rel-momentum} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \rho_0\!\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}
    + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\right)
  = -\nabla p,
  \label{eq:nr-euler}
\end{equation}
which is Euler's equation as used throughout Chandrasekhar's book.

\noindent
\textbf{Dissipative terms:}  The BDNK constitutive relations reduce
directly to the standard constitutive laws of Newtonian fluid
mechanics in the non-relativistic limit:
\begin{align}
  \Pi^{ij} &\to -\shearvisc\!\left(\partial_i v_j + \partial_j v_i
    - \tfrac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}\right),
  \label{eq:nr-shear}\\[4pt]
  \Pi_{\mathrm{bulk}} &\to -\bulkvisc\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v},
  \label{eq:nr-bulk}\\[4pt]
  Q^i &\to -\kappa\,\partial_i T,
  \label{eq:nr-fourier}
\end{align}
since the BDNK frame corrections are of order $O(v^2/c^2)$ and
vanish in the non-relativistic limit, recovering the Navier--Stokes
equations without requiring any relaxation-time limit.

\subsection{Relativistic correction terms at $O(v^2/c^2)$}
\label{sec:rel-corrections}

Retaining the first relativistic corrections, the momentum equation
becomes
\begin{equation}
  \rho_0\!\left(1 + \frac{e + p/\rho_0}{c^2}\right)
    \!\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}
    + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\right)
  = -\nabla p
    - \frac{\mathbf{v}}{c^2}\,\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}
    + O(v^4/c^4).
  \label{eq:post-newtonian-momentum}
\end{equation}
The two correction terms are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The factor $(1 + (e + p/\rho_0)/c^2)$ enhancing the
  effective inertia (pressure and internal energy contribute to
  mass--energy).
\item The $-(\mathbf{v}/c^2)\,\partial_t p$ term, which couples the
  time derivative of pressure to the momentum equation---absent in
  the Newtonian theory.
\end{enumerate}
These corrections become significant when $p/(\rho_0 c^2) \gtrsim 0.01$,
as occurs in neutron star interiors.

\subsection{Emergence of Chandrasekhar's equations}
\label{sec:chandrasekhar-limit}

Adding the gravitational acceleration $\mathbf{g}$ and the viscous
stress tensor $\tau_{ij}$ in the Newtonian limit, we recover
precisely Chandrasekhar's starting point (Chapter~I of the original
text):
\begin{align}
  \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
    + \nabla\cdot(\rho\,\mathbf{v}) &= 0,
  \label{eq:chandra-cont}\\[4pt]
  \rho\!\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}
    + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\right)
  &= -\nabla p + \rho\,\mathbf{g}
    + \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau},
  \label{eq:chandra-mom}\\[4pt]
  \rho T\!\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial t}
    + \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla s\right)
  &= \nabla\cdot(\kappa\,\nabla T)
    + \boldsymbol{\tau}:\nabla\mathbf{v}.
  \label{eq:chandra-entropy}
\end{align}
Every stability analysis in the original book departs from these
equations.  The relativistic generalizations derived above replace
them when $v/c$ or $p/(\rho c^2)$ is not negligible.

\section{The Gavassino Theorem: Thermodynamic Stability Implies Causality}
\label{sec:gavassino-stability-causality}
%% ====================================================================

A foundational result that underpins the entire programme of this book
was established by Gavassino~\citep{GavassinoCausality2021}: if a
relativistic hydrodynamic theory possesses an entropy current
$s^{\mu}$ satisfying $\nabla_{\mu} s^{\mu} \ge 0$, and the entropy is
maximized at equilibrium (Gibbs stability), then perturbations about
equilibrium \emph{cannot propagate outside the light cone}.  In other
words, thermodynamic stability alone guarantees causality.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=red!5!white, colframe=red!60!black,
  title={\textbf{Theorem (Gavassino 2021): Stability $\Rightarrow$ Causality}},
  fonttitle=\bfseries]
\label{thm:gavassino-stability-causality}
Let $\varphi^{A}$ denote the linearised perturbation fields about a
homogeneous equilibrium state, obeying first-order evolution equations
\begin{equation}
  M^{\mu}_{AB}\,\partial_{\mu}\varphi^{B} = -\Xi_{AB}\,\varphi^{B}\,,
  \label{eq:gavassino-eom}
\end{equation}
with $M^{0}_{AB}$ invertible.  Suppose there exist constant symmetric
matrices $E^{\mu}_{AB}$ and $\sigma_{AB}$ such that the
\emph{information current}
\begin{equation}
  E^{\mu} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,E^{\mu}_{AB}\,\varphi^{A}\,\varphi^{B}
  \label{eq:gavassino-info-current}
\end{equation}
is future-directed timelike for all non-vanishing perturbations, and
the entropy production rate satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{\mu} E^{\mu} = -\sigma_{AB}\,\varphi^{A}\,\varphi^{B}
  \;\le\; 0\,.
  \label{eq:gavassino-entropy-prod}
\end{equation}
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{(I)}] The system~\eqref{eq:gavassino-eom} is
    \emph{causal}: perturbations propagate within the light cone.
  \item[\textbf{(II)}] The system can be rewritten in
    \emph{symmetric-hyperbolic} form:
    \begin{equation}
      E^{\mu}_{AB}\,\partial_{\mu}\varphi^{B}
      = -\sigma_{AB}\,\varphi^{B}
        - \Xi_{[AB]}\,\varphi^{B}\,,
      \label{eq:gavassino-symhyp}
    \end{equation}
    which is manifestly well-posed (existence, uniqueness, continuous
    dependence on data).
\end{enumerate}
\end{tcolorbox}

\noindent
The \emph{information current} $E^{\mu}$ is constructed as
$E^{\mu} = \Phi_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\mu} - \Phi^{\mu}$, where
$\Phi^{\mu}$ is the grand-canonical potential current and
$\Phi_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\mu}$ its equilibrium value.  Its time component
$E^{0}$ is the Gibbs stability functional: positive definiteness of
$E^{0}$ is precisely the statement that the equilibrium state maximises
the entropy at fixed energy and particle number
\citep{GavassinoGibbs2021}.

\subsubsection{Significance for this book}
This theorem has three profound consequences for the stability
analyses throughout this book:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{BDNK and IS are both covered.}  The theorem applies to
    any first-order system with a positive-definite information current.
    Both the BDNK first-order causal theory
    \citep{BemficaDN2018,Kovtun2019} and the Israel--Stewart
    second-order theory \citep{IsraelStewart1979} satisfy the
    hypotheses, so their causality follows from a single unified
    argument.

  \item \textbf{Stability checks suffice.}  When verifying that a
    particular equilibrium configuration (e.g., a stratified neutron
    star layer) supports causal perturbation propagation, it is
    \emph{sufficient} to verify the Gibbs stability conditions
    ($E^{0}_{AB}$ positive definite and $\sigma_{AB}$ positive
    semidefinite).  There is no need to separately check characteristic
    speeds.

  \item \textbf{Universality.}  Since the theorem depends only on the
    existence of an entropy current and Gibbs stability---not on the
    specific constitutive relations---it provides a
    \emph{formalism-independent} guarantee that all thermodynamically
    consistent relativistic hydrodynamic theories are causal in the
    linear regime.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Complementary results}

Gavassino's programme extends this stability--causality connection in
several directions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gibbs stability criterion}
    \citep{GavassinoGibbs2021}: A systematic method to construct the
    Lyapunov functional $E = -\delta S$ for any relativistic fluid
    theory from the maximum entropy principle.  The functional is
    guaranteed to be non-increasing by the second law.

  \item \textbf{Entropy has no maximum (Eckart/Landau--Lifshitz
    instability)} \citep{GavassinoLyapunov2020}: The instability of
    the Eckart and Landau--Lifshitz first-order theories is traced to
    the fact that the total entropy, restricted to dynamically
    accessible states, has no upper bound---the truncation of the
    entropy current at first order converts the maximum into a saddle
    point.

  \item \textbf{Onsager--Casimir and symmetric hyperbolicity}
    \citep{GavassinoCasimir2022}: The Onsager--Casimir reciprocal
    relations impose symmetry conditions on the principal part of the
    linearised equations, ensuring symmetric hyperbolicity in the
    absence of macroscopic magnetic fields.

  \item \textbf{Lorentz invariance of entropy}
    \citep{GavassinoLorentz2021}: The thermodynamic entropy $S$ is
    proven to be a Lorentz scalar from first principles (field theory
    + second law in one frame), without assuming covariance of
    thermodynamics a priori.  This grounds the entire relativistic
    thermodynamic framework used in this book.
\end{itemize}

\section{Gavassino's Universality Classes and Formalism Independence}
\label{sec:gavassino-universality}
%% ====================================================================

A second major result, established by Gavassino, Disconzi \&
Noronha~\citep{GavassinoUniversality1,GavassinoUniversality2},
resolves a long-standing question in relativistic hydrodynamics: do
Israel--Stewart (IS) and BDNK give the same physical predictions?

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=green!5!white, colframe=green!50!black,
  title={\textbf{Universality Theorem (Gavassino, Disconzi \& Noronha
    2023)}},
  fonttitle=\bfseries]
\label{thm:universality}
Two relativistic hydrodynamic theories belong to the same
\emph{universality class} if they have the same degrees of freedom
(classified by their $SO(3)$ transformation properties), the same
conservation laws, and a thermodynamically stable equilibrium.  All
theories in the same universality class are \emph{mathematically
equivalent} when linearised about homogeneous equilibrium: there
exists a change of variables mapping the equations of one theory
into those of the other.

In particular, \textbf{all second-order viscous hydrodynamic theories}
(IS, BRSSS, DNMR, Chapman--Enskog, etc.)\ \textbf{and the BDNK
first-order theory are equivalent in the linear regime} when they
share the same universality class
$(\mathfrak{g}_{0},\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{2})
-(\bar{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},\bar{\mathfrak{g}}_{1},
\bar{\mathfrak{g}}_{2})$.
\end{tcolorbox}

\noindent
\textbf{Consequence for critical parameters.}
Since the onset of hydrodynamic instabilities (convection, rotation,
magnetic field effects) is determined by the \emph{marginal state}
$\sigma = 0$ of the linearised equations, and all theories in the same
universality class have identical linearised equations (up to a change
of variables), it follows that:

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white, colframe=blue!50!black,
  title={\textbf{Formalism-Independence of Critical Parameters}},
  fonttitle=\bfseries]
\label{box:formalism-independence}
The critical Rayleigh number $\mathrm{Ra}_{c}$, critical Taylor
number $\mathrm{Ta}_{c}$, critical Chandrasekhar number $Q_{c}$, and
all other critical parameters derived in this book are
\emph{independent of the choice of dissipative formalism} (BDNK vs.\
IS vs.\ any other second-order theory).  The only differences between
formalisms appear in the \emph{non-hydrodynamic sector}---transient
relaxation modes that lie outside the domain of validity of
hydrodynamics.
\end{tcolorbox}

\noindent
This result justifies our use of the BDNK formalism throughout the
book without loss of generality: every critical threshold we compute
would be obtained identically using Israel--Stewart or any other
thermodynamically consistent theory.  See the accompanying paper
\citep{GavassinoUniversality2} for explicit demonstrations that
Hydro+ $\equiv$ IS (bulk viscosity), superfluid hydrodynamics
$\equiv$ IS (heat conduction, $\kappa \to \infty$), and many other
surprising equivalences.

\section{Relativistic Boussinesq Approximation}
\label{sec:rel-boussinesq}
%% ====================================================================

\subsection{Classical Boussinesq approximation}
\label{sec:classical-boussinesq}

In the classical (Newtonian) Boussinesq approximation, density
variations are neglected everywhere except in the buoyancy term.
One writes $\rho = \rho_0(1 - \alpha\,\delta T)$, where
$\alpha = -\rho_0^{-1}(\partial\rho/\partial T)_p$ is the thermal
expansion coefficient, and the fluid is treated as incompressible
($\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$) in all other terms.

\subsection{Relativistic generalization}
\label{sec:rel-boussinesq-gen}

In a relativistic fluid, the key modification is that \emph{pressure
contributes to the inertial mass density}: the Newtonian $\rho$ is
replaced by $\enthalpy = (\varepsilon + p)/c^2$ in the momentum
equation.  The relativistic Boussinesq approximation therefore
proceeds as follows.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 1: Decompose the enthalpy density.}
\begin{equation}
  \enthalpy = \enthalpy_0 + \delta\enthalpy,
  \qquad
  \enthalpy_0 = \frac{\varepsilon_0 + p_0}{c^2},
  \label{eq:rel-bouss-decomp}
\end{equation}
where $\delta\enthalpy$ contains contributions from both thermal and
pressure perturbations:
\begin{equation}
  \delta\enthalpy
    = \frac{\delta\varepsilon + \delta p}{c^2}
    = \frac{1}{c^2}\!\left[
      \left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial T}\right)_{\!p} \delta T
      + \left(1 + \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial p}\bigg|_T\right) \delta p
    \right].
  \label{eq:rel-dw}
\end{equation}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 2: Define the relativistic thermal expansion coefficient.}
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{\mathrm{rel}}
    = -\frac{1}{\enthalpy_0}
      \left(\frac{\partial\enthalpy}{\partial T}\right)_{\!p}
    = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 + p_0}
      \left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial T}\right)_{\!p}.
  \label{eq:rel-alpha}
\end{equation}
In the non-relativistic limit, $\varepsilon \approx \rho c^2$,
so $\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}} \to -(\partial\rho/\partial T)_p / \rho_0
= \alpha$, recovering the classical coefficient.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 3: Relativistic Boussinesq equations.}

Applying the Boussinesq approximation---neglecting $\delta\enthalpy$
everywhere except in the buoyancy term, and imposing the
incompressibility condition $\partial_i\,\delta v^i = 0$---the
linearized relativistic equations become:
\begin{align}
  \partial_i\,\delta v^i &= 0,
  \label{eq:rel-bouss-div}\\[4pt]
  \enthalpy_0\,\frac{\partial\,\delta v^i}{\partial t}
  &= -\partial_i\,\delta p
    + \delta\enthalpy\, g^i
    + \enthalpy_0\,\nu\,\nabla^2\delta v^i,
  \label{eq:rel-bouss-mom}\\[4pt]
  \frac{\partial\,\delta T}{\partial t}
  &= \kappa_T\,\nabla^2\delta T
    - \delta v^i\,\partial_i T_0,
  \label{eq:rel-bouss-temp}
\end{align}
where $\nu = \shearvisc/\enthalpy_0$ is the relativistic kinematic
viscosity and $\kappa_T$ is the thermal diffusivity.

The buoyancy term in \eqref{eq:rel-bouss-mom} is
\begin{equation}
  \delta\enthalpy\, g^i
    = -\enthalpy_0\,\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}}\,\delta T\, g^i
      + \frac{\delta p}{c^2}\, g^i.
  \label{eq:rel-buoyancy}
\end{equation}
The second term, $\delta p\, g^i / c^2$, is purely relativistic
and represents the gravitational coupling of pressure
perturbations.  It is absent in Chandrasekhar's classical treatment
and becomes important when $p_0/(\rho_0 c^2)$ is not negligible.

\subsection{Relativistic Rayleigh number}
\label{sec:rel-rayleigh}

Defining the Rayleigh number in terms of the relativistic quantities:
\begin{equation}
  \Ra_{\mathrm{rel}}
    = \frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}}\, g\, d^3\,\Delta T}%
      {\nu\,\kappa_T}
    = \frac{\alpha\, g\, d^3\,\Delta T}{\nu\,\kappa_T}
      \left[1 + O\!\left(\frac{p_0}{\rho_0 c^2}\right)\right],
  \label{eq:rel-rayleigh}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the layer depth and $\Delta T$ the imposed temperature
difference.  The relativistic correction enters through
$\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}} \neq \alpha$ and through the redefinition
of kinematic viscosity $\nu = \shearvisc/\enthalpy_0$ rather than
$\shearvisc/\rho_0$.

\subsection{Implications for stability thresholds}
\label{sec:bouss-implications}

The critical Rayleigh number $\Ra_c$ for the onset of convection is
unchanged in form (it is determined by the eigenvalue problem for
the linearized equations), but its \emph{value in terms of physical
parameters} differs:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The replacement $\rho_0 \to \enthalpy_0 = (\varepsilon_0 + p_0)/c^2$
  increases the effective inertia, making the fluid harder to
  destabilize for a given temperature gradient.
\item The pressure-buoyancy coupling [$\delta p\, g/c^2$ in
  \eqref{eq:rel-buoyancy}] provides an additional destabilizing
  mechanism absent in the classical theory.
\item The modified thermal expansion $\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}}$ can be
  significantly different from $\alpha$ in relativistic regimes.
\end{enumerate}
These effects are quantified for specific physical systems in the
subsequent chapters.

\section{BDNK Frame Coefficient Constraints}
\label{sec:bdnk-parameter-space}
%% ====================================================================

The practical implementation of BDNK hydrodynamics requires an explicit
choice of frame coefficients $(\varepsilon_1, \beta_1, \alpha_1)$ that
simultaneously guarantees strong hyperbolicity (causality), stability of
the equilibrium state, and well-posedness of the initial-value problem.
This section maps out the allowed parameter space, drawing on recent
analytical and numerical results
\citep{PandyaPretorius2021,PandyaMostPretorius2022ideal,HoultKovtun2022kinetic,BantilanIshiRomatschke2018}.

\subsection{Dimensionless frame parameters}
\label{sec:dimless-frame}

For a single-component fluid, the relevant dimensionless combinations
are
\begin{equation}
  a_E \equiv \frac{\varepsilon_1\, T}{\shearvisc},
  \qquad
  a_\beta \equiv \frac{\beta_1}{\shearvisc},
  \label{eq:dimless-frame-params}
\end{equation}
where $\shearvisc$ is the shear viscosity and $T$ the temperature.
The strong hyperbolicity conditions, derived from the requirement that
all eigenvalues of the principal symbol of the BDNK system be real and
distinct, take the form of coupled nonlinear inequalities in
$(a_E, a_\beta)$ that depend on the equation of state through the
sound speed $\cs$.

\subsection{Hyperbolicity region for ideal gas}
\label{sec:hyper-ideal-gas}

For an ideal gas with adiabatic index $\hat{\gamma}$ and sound speed
$\cs^2 = \hat{\gamma}\, p/(\varepsilon + p)$, the principal constraints
are \citep{BemficaDNK2023,HoultKovtun2022kinetic}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Minimum energy coefficient:}
  \begin{equation}
    a_E > \frac{\cs^2/c^2}{1 - \cs^2/c^2}.
    \label{eq:aE-min}
  \end{equation}
  For a conformal fluid ($\cs^2 = c^2/3$), this gives $a_E > 1/2$.
  For stiff nuclear matter ($\cs^2 \approx 0.7\, c^2$), the bound
  tightens considerably to $a_E > 7/3$.

\item \textbf{Coupled bound on $a_\beta$:}
  \begin{equation}
    a_\beta > \frac{(\cs^2/c^2)\, a_E}%
      {a_E\,(1 - \cs^2/c^2) - \cs^2/c^2}\,,
    \label{eq:abeta-bound}
  \end{equation}
  valid when $a_E$ exceeds the threshold in \eqref{eq:aE-min}.
  This hyperbolic curve defines the lower boundary of the allowed
  region in the $(a_E, a_\beta)$ plane.

\item \textbf{Subluminality upper bound:} An upper bound on $a_\beta$
  from the requirement that all characteristic speeds remain below $c$:
  \begin{equation}
    a_\beta < \frac{(1 - \cs^2/c^2)\, a_E + 2\,(1 - \cs^2/c^2)}%
      {\cs^2/c^2}.
    \label{eq:abeta-upper}
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

The allowed region is thus a strip bounded below by the hyperbolicity
curve \eqref{eq:abeta-bound} and above by the subluminality
bound \eqref{eq:abeta-upper}, with $a_E$ bounded below by
\eqref{eq:aE-min}.  As the sound speed increases (stiffer EOS),
the allowed region narrows---reflecting the greater difficulty of
maintaining causality in stiff matter.

\subsection{Connection to $\shearvisc/s$ and the KSS bound}
\label{sec:kss-connection}

The Kovtun--Son--Starinets (KSS) bound \citep{KSS2005} from the
AdS/CFT correspondence provides a lower limit on the shear viscosity
to entropy density ratio:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\shearvisc}{s} \ge \frac{1}{4\pi}\,.
  \label{eq:kss-bound}
\end{equation}
This bound has been conjectured to hold universally for all
relativistic quantum field theories at finite temperature.  Together
with the positivity of bulk viscosity ($\bulkvisc \ge 0$), the KSS
bound constrains the transport coefficient space for BDNK applications.

For neutron star matter, the transport coefficients depend strongly on
the baryon density through nuclear interactions, with $\shearvisc/s$
typically well above the KSS bound at the low temperatures relevant to
cold neutron stars, but potentially approaching it in the hot, dense
conditions of binary mergers and proto-neutron stars
\citep{AlfordMahmoodi2012,GavassinoAntonelli2024}.

\subsection{Numerical BDNK implementations}
\label{sec:bdnk-numerics}

The first numerical implementations of BDNK hydrodynamics were
performed by Pandya \& Pretorius (2021) \citep{PandyaPretorius2021},
who demonstrated stable evolution of shock tubes and blast waves in
1+1 dimensions.  Subsequent work by Pandya, Most \& Pretorius (2022)
\citep{PandyaMostPretorius2022ideal} extended these results to ideal gas
microphysics, validating the hyperbolicity conditions \eqref{eq:aE-min}--\eqref{eq:abeta-upper} and confirming that the
frame coefficients can be chosen to give stable, convergent
evolutions across a wide range of flow regimes.

Bantilan, Ishi \& Romatschke (2018) \citep{BantilanIshiRomatschke2018}
applied BDNK-type first-order hydrodynamics to predict Centauro-like
events in heavy-ion collisions at the LHC, demonstrating the
phenomenological utility of the framework.

The recent application of BDNK viscous hydrodynamics to neutron star
evolution \citep{NSevolutionBDNK2025} extends the framework to
long-timescale astrophysical problems, showing that the BDNK frame
coefficients can be chosen to maintain stability over secular
evolution timescales while correctly reproducing the known cooling
and spin-down behaviour.

\section{Information Current and Fluctuating BDNK Hydrodynamics}
\label{sec:info-current-fluctuations}
%% ====================================================================

A central advance in the theoretical underpinning of first-order causal
hydrodynamics is the construction of an \emph{information current}
$E^{\mu}$ within the BDNK framework
\citep{GavassinoInfoCurrentBDNK2024}.  The information current quantifies
the net flow of thermodynamic information carried by perturbations around
equilibrium.  Given the entropy current $s^{\mu}$ and conserved currents
$J^{I\mu}$, it is defined as
\begin{equation}
  E^{\mu} = -\delta s^{\mu} - \alpha_{I}^{*}\,\delta J^{I\mu}\,,
  \label{eq:info-current-def}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{I}^{*}$ are the equilibrium values of the thermodynamic
conjugates and $\delta$ denotes a finite perturbation from equilibrium.
The free energy variation is then
$\delta\Omega = T\!\int_{\Sigma}\! d\Sigma\, n_{\mu}\,E^{\mu}$,
and the equilibrium probability distribution for fluctuations is
$p[\delta\phi^{A}] \propto \exp\!\bigl(-\!\int\! d\Sigma\, n_{\mu}\,E^{\mu}\bigr)$.

\subsection{Role in stability and causality}

A relativistic system is linearly causal and stable against fluctuations
if \citep{GavassinoInfoCurrentBDNK2024}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $n_{\mu}\,E^{\mu} \ge 0$ for any past-directed timelike $n^{\mu}$
    (i.e., $E^{\mu}$ is timelike future-directed);
  \item The bound $n_{\mu}\,E^{\mu} = 0$ is saturated only if
    $\delta\phi^{A} = 0$ (equilibrium is unique);
  \item The second law $\partial_{\mu}E^{\mu} \le 0$ holds.
\end{enumerate}
In the BDNK framework, the construction of $E^{\mu}$ requires
UV-regularization: the first-order truncation of the entropy current
does not produce a timelike $E^{\mu}$ at high gradients, so
additional second-order terms of the form $\partial\varphi\,\partial\varphi$
(which lie within the truncation error) must be tuned to make
$E^{\mu}$ timelike future-directed also in the UV.  This regularization
is always possible for the physically relevant BDNK theories
\citep{GavassinoInfoCurrentBDNK2024}.

\subsection{Fluctuating BDNK theory}

Building on the information current, Gavassino et~al.\
\citep{GavassinoBDNKFluctuations2024} constructed the first
mathematically consistent theory of stochastic fluctuations within
BDNK hydrodynamics.  The key result is that the noise correlators
are guaranteed to be observer-independent for thermodynamically
stable models: the Martin--Siggia--Rose (MSR) effective action is
well-defined and the fluctuation-dissipation theorem is satisfied in a
manifestly covariant manner.

The linearized equations of motion take the form
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(E_{AB}^{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu} + \sigma_{AB}\bigr)\,\delta\phi^{B}
  = \xi_{A}\,,
  \label{eq:fluctuating-eom}
\end{equation}
where $E^{\mu} = \delta\phi^{A}\,E_{AB}^{\mu}\,\delta\phi^{B}/2$,
$\sigma = \delta\phi^{A}\,\sigma_{AB}\,\delta\phi^{B}$ is the entropy
production, and $\xi_{A}$ is a Gaussian stochastic source with zero
mean and correlator
\begin{equation}
  \langle\xi_{A}(x)\,\xi_{B}(x')\rangle
  = 2\,\sigma_{AB}\,\delta^{(4)}(x - x')\,.
  \label{eq:noise-correlator}
\end{equation}
The MSR action for the fluctuating theory is generically bilocal and
the noise is not white; however, correlators of conserved densities
remain spatially localized and coincide with those computed in
fluctuating Israel--Stewart theory
\citep{GavassinoBDNKFluctuations2024,MullinsHippertGavassinoNoronha2024}.

The effective action approach of Mullins et~al.\
\citep{MullinsHippertGavassinoNoronha2024,MullinsGavassinoStochastic2024}
further reveals a $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$ symmetry, analogous to the
Kubo--Martin--Schwinger (KMS) symmetry, involving time reversal and
parity, which implements the principle of detailed balance in a
covariant manner.  This symmetry provides a new guide for constructing
effective actions for hydrodynamic systems whose dynamics are not solely
governed by conservation laws.

\section{Regime of Applicability of Israel--Stewart Theory}
\label{sec:rel-regime-IS}
%% ====================================================================

A central question for every chapter in this book is: when can we trust
Israel--Stewart (IS) hydrodynamics, and when must we rely on the
first-order BDNK framework?  Wagner \& Gavassino
(\citealt{WagnerGavassinoRegime2024}, arXiv:2309.14828) have provided a
systematic answer using linear-response theory.

\subsection{The ranking of IS truncations}

Consider a fluid element with internal non-equilibrium degrees of freedom
$\mathbb{A} = \{\mathbb{A}^1, \mathbb{A}^2, \ldots\}$ driven by a
gradient $\theta(t)$.  The exact linear-response Green function is a sum
of exponentials:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-Green-exact}
G(t) = \Theta(t)\sum_n \frac{\zeta_n}{\tau_n}\,e^{-t/\tau_n}\,,
\end{equation}
where $\tau_n$ are the microscopic relaxation times and $\zeta_n$ the
associated viscous susceptibilities.  Israel--Stewart replaces this with
a single relaxation:
$G_{\mathrm{IS}}(t) = \Theta(t)(\zeta/\tau_\Pi)\,e^{-t/\tau_\Pi}$.
The question is how to choose $\tau_\Pi$ and $\zeta$.  Wagner \& Gavassino
compare six prescriptions, ranking them in decreasing accuracy:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{IReD} (Inverse Reynolds Dominance): $\zeta = \sum_n \zeta_n$,
  $\tau_\Pi = \sum_n \zeta_n \tau_n / \sum_m \zeta_m$.  This is the
  susceptibility-weighted average of relaxation times, and is accurate
  both in the asymptotic (Navier--Stokes) regime and in the transient regime.
\item \textbf{DNMR}: $\tau_\Pi = \max\{\tau_n\}$.  Accurate at late
  times but overestimates the transient timescale when the slowest mode
  has small susceptibility.
\item \textbf{Second-order gradient expansion} (non-resummed BRSSS):
  Often \emph{less} accurate than Navier--Stokes in the transient regime.
\item \textbf{14-moment approximation}: Least accurate overall.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Criterion for book chapters}

The key finding is that \emph{IS hydrodynamics is falsifiable}: the
relaxation time $\tau_\Pi$ is an observable quantity (not merely a UV
regulator), and the IReD prescription gives the optimal match to the
exact microscopic dynamics.  For each stability problem in this book,
the IS correction to the perturbation equations is physically meaningful
whenever the driving force $\theta(t)$ varies on timescales comparable
to $\tau_\Pi$.  When $\theta$ varies slowly compared to all $\tau_n$,
Navier--Stokes (and hence BDNK) suffices.

\section{Convergence and Divergence of the Hydrodynamic Gradient Expansion}
\label{sec:rel-gradient-expansion}
%% ====================================================================

The gradient expansion
$T^{\mu\nu}(\mathrm{Kn}) = T^{\mu\nu}_{\mathrm{ID}} + T^{\mu\nu}_1\,
\mathrm{Kn} + T^{\mu\nu}_2\,\mathrm{Kn}^2 + \cdots$
is the foundation upon which Navier--Stokes, Burnett, and higher-order
hydrodynamics are constructed.  Two recent works by Gavassino clarify
its convergence properties.

\subsection{Infinite-order hydrodynamics (Gavassino, arXiv:2402.19343)}

Gavassino (\citealt{GavassinoInfiniteOrder2024}) constructs a
radiation-hydrodynamic model where the gradient expansion can be computed
analytically to \emph{all} orders.  The key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The gradient series is \emph{factorially divergent} for most flows,
  with coefficients growing as $(n+3)!$ at order $n$.  The divergence
  originates from particles with arbitrarily long mean free paths
  (high-energy tails of the kinetic distribution).

\item A UV cutoff $\Lambda$ on particle energies regularizes the series,
  restoring convergence when $\Lambda/T < \mathrm{Kn}^{-1}$.  The
  regularized series agrees with the exact non-local constitutive
  relation to exponential accuracy.

\item The breakdown of the regularized gradient expansion occurs at
  $\mathrm{Kn} \sim 0.1$---precisely where Navier--Stokes ceases to
  hold.  Thus, higher-derivative corrections do not extend the regime
  of applicability of hydrodynamics beyond first order; they merely
  refine accuracy within the same domain.

\item All odd-order truncations of the gradient series are \emph{elliptic}
  (acausal, unstable, ill-posed), confirming that only even-order
  truncations (Navier--Stokes, Burnett, etc.) are physically meaningful.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Convergence criterion (Gavassino, arXiv:2408.14316)}

Gavassino (\citealt{GavassinoConvergence2024}) provides rigorous
convergence theorems for hydrodynamic dispersion relations in relativistic
kinetic theory:

\begin{theorem}[Gavassino 2024]
If the non-hydrodynamic sector has a finite gap $\omega_g = -i/\tau_g$
at zero wavenumber, then the Taylor series of all hydrodynamic dispersion
relations has a non-vanishing radius of convergence.  For shear waves,
$R_{\mathrm{shear}} \geq (2\tau_g)^{-1}$.
\end{theorem}

The gap exists whenever the total scattering cross-section is bounded
below by a positive constant at all energies ($\inf_s \sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}
(s) = \sigma_0 > 0$).  Combined with the gaplessness criterion of
Gavassino (\citealt{GavassinoGapless2024}, arXiv:2404.12327)---which
proves that the non-hydrodynamic sector is gapless whenever the
cross-section vanishes at high energies---these results provide a
complete picture: standard QFT cross-sections (which decay at high
energy) lead to gapless non-hydrodynamic sectors and divergent gradient
expansions, while hard-sphere-type interactions preserve a gap and
convergence.

\subsection{Stochastic fluctuations in kinetic theory (Rocha, Gavassino,
  Mullins; arXiv:2405.10878)}

Rocha, Gavassino \& Mullins (\citealt{RochaGavassinoFluctu2024})
develop a Lorentz-covariant framework for equilibrium fluctuations in
relativistic kinetic theory using Gavassino's information current.  The
resulting stochastic theory is proven causal and covariantly stable,
with predictions independent of the spacetime foliation used to define
grand-canonical probabilities.  Key results relevant to this book:
\begin{enumerate}
\item In a box with $N > 5$ particles, Boltzmann's molecular chaos
  postulate breaks with probability $\sim N^{-1/2}$, leading to a
  breakdown of the Boltzmann equation in small systems.
\item Transient (Israel--Stewart-type) hydrodynamics already accounts
  for at least 80\% of the equilibrium fluctuations of the stress-energy
  tensor at any given time---a strong validation of the IS framework.
\item For systems with energy-dependent mean free paths that diverge at
  high energy, dehydrodynamization of the stochastic theory occurs: the
  fluctuation correlation functions develop long tails that decay as
  power laws rather than exponentials, mirroring the gapless
  non-hydrodynamic modes of Gavassino (2024).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Spin Hydrodynamics: The Perfect Spinfluid}
\label{sec:spin-hydro}

\begin{relcorrection}
\textbf{Divergence-type spin hydrodynamics.}
In relativistic heavy-ion collisions, the quark--gluon plasma can carry
significant spin polarisation, requiring hydrodynamic frameworks that
include spin degrees of freedom alongside the usual energy, momentum,
and charge conservation.  Abboud, Gavassino, Singh \&
Speranza~\cite{AbboudGavassinoSinghSperanzaSpinfluid2025} have
incorporated spin into the divergence-type theory framework, yielding
the first non-dissipative spin hydrodynamic theory with guaranteed
non-linear causality and symmetric hyperbolicity.

The construction proceeds as follows.  For a perfect spinfluid, both
the energy--momentum tensor $T^{\mu\nu}$ (symmetric) and the spin
current $\mathcal{S}^{\lambda\mu\nu}$ (antisymmetric in $\mu\nu$)
are independently conserved:
\begin{equation}
  \partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0, \qquad
  \partial_\lambda \mathcal{S}^{\lambda\mu\nu} = 0.
  \label{eq:spinfluid-conservation}
\end{equation}
The theory has $1 + 4 + 6 = 11$ independent conservation laws
(particle number, energy--momentum, and spin), matched by 11
independent thermodynamic conjugate fields: fugacity $\alpha$,
inverse-temperature four-vector $\beta_\mu$, and antisymmetric spin
potential $\Omega_{\mu\nu}$.

The Massieu current $\chi^\lambda = s^\lambda + \zeta_A N^{\lambda A}$
serves as the generating function: its derivatives with respect to the
conjugate fields yield all constitutive relations, and the resulting
system is automatically symmetric hyperbolic whenever the generating
function is strictly convex.  For a specific realisation based on spin
kinetic theory (extending the Maxwell--J\"uttner distribution to
include spin), the authors prove that causality and symmetric
hyperbolicity hold to all orders in the spin potential, provided a
thermodynamic constraint on the spin potential magnitude is satisfied.

This framework extends the divergence-type structure---which underpins
both the BDNK formalism and the stability--causality connection---to
systems with additional (spin) degrees of freedom, demonstrating that
the principle ``stability implies causality'' extends to the spin
sector.
\end{relcorrection}

\section{Non-Perturbative Heat Conduction: GENERIC--Carter Correspondence}
\label{sec:generic-carter}

\begin{relcorrection}
\textbf{Mapping GENERIC to Carter's multifluid theory.}
The BDNK and Israel--Stewart theories described above are both derived
from near-equilibrium expansions.  For phenomena involving large heat
fluxes---such as the cooling of newly-born neutron stars or
post-merger remnants---a non-perturbative theory of heat conduction is
desirable.  Gavassino~\cite{GavassinoGENERICCarter2023} has established
an exact correspondence between the GENERIC model for relativistic heat
conduction (developed by \"Ottinger) and Carter's two-fluid model,
where entropy and particles form the two interpenetrating fluids.

The stress--energy tensor in Carter's formulation takes the form
\begin{equation}
  T^{\alpha\beta} = P\,g^{\alpha\beta}
    + \tilde{C}\,s^\alpha s^\beta
    + \tilde{A}(s^\alpha n^\beta + n^\alpha s^\beta)
    + \tilde{B}\,n^\alpha n^\beta,
  \label{eq:carter-two-fluid}
\end{equation}
where $s^\alpha$ and $n^\alpha$ are the entropy and particle currents,
and $P$, $\tilde{A}$, $\tilde{B}$, $\tilde{C}$ are functions
determined by the GENERIC generating functional.  The key insight is
that the change of variables mapping GENERIC into Carter's framework
is \emph{exact} (not perturbative), allowing one to derive Carter's
constitutive relations directly from GENERIC's structure.

A remarkable consequence is that, in the limit of infinite thermal
conductivity ($\kappa \to \infty$), GENERIC heat conduction reduces
to the relativistic two-fluid model for superfluidity.  This
``crossover'' occurs because causality forces the diffusing entropy
to accumulate on the lightcone surface, where it travels at the
speed of light like a wave rather than diffusing.  This connects
the dissipative framework of this chapter to the superfluid dynamics
relevant for Chapters~X--XII.
\end{relcorrection}
