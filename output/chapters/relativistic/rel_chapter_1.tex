\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:rel-1-1}

The equations of relativistic hydrodynamics---like their
non-relativistic counterparts---admit simple stationary flow
patterns whose realizability is limited to certain ranges of the
governing parameters.  Outside these ranges the flows are
\emph{unstable}: they cannot sustain themselves against small
perturbations.  The demarcation of stable from unstable states
constitutes the subject of \emph{relativistic hydrodynamic stability}.

When the characteristic velocities in the problem approach the speed
of light~$c$, or when thermal and internal energies become comparable
to the rest-mass energy, the Navier--Stokes description of viscous
flow must be replaced by a manifestly covariant framework.  Two
physical requirements distinguish the relativistic theory from the
classical one:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Covariance.}  All equations are tensor equations on
  spacetime, written in terms of the energy-momentum tensor
  $T^{\mu\nu}$, the baryon-number current $n\, u^{\mu}$, and
  (when electromagnetic fields are present) the Faraday tensor
  $F^{\mu\nu}$.
\item \textbf{Causality.}  No signal---including perturbation modes,
  thermal waves, and viscous diffusion fronts---may propagate faster
  than~$c$.  This requirement rules out parabolic transport equations
  (Fourier, Navier--Stokes) in favour of hyperbolic, causal
  alternatives.  The BDNK (Bemfica--Disconzi--Noronha--Kovtun) theory
  achieves this with first-order constitutive relations by working in
  a general hydrodynamic frame whose transport coefficients are
  constrained to ensure strong hyperbolicity
  \cite{BemficaDN2018,Kovtun2019}.
\end{enumerate}

In what follows we reformulate the basic concepts of Chapter~I in
this relativistic setting.  Throughout, Greek indices
$\mu,\nu,\ldots$ run over $0,1,2,3$ with signature $(-,+,+,+)$;
Latin indices $i,j,\ldots$ label spatial directions $1,2,3$.  The
speed of light~$c$ is kept explicit so that the non-relativistic limit
$v/c\to 0$ is transparent.  The four-velocity is normalised as
$u^{\mu}u_{\mu}=-c^{2}$, and the projection tensor orthogonal to the
flow is $\Delta^{\mu\nu}=g^{\mu\nu}+u^{\mu}u^{\nu}/c^{2}$.

\section{Relativistic fluid equations}
\label{sec:rel-1-2}

\subsection{Perfect-fluid (Euler) sector}
\label{sec:rel-1-2a}

For a perfect fluid the energy-momentum tensor is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-1}
T^{\mu\nu} = \frac{\varepsilon + p}{c^{2}}\,u^{\mu}\,u^{\nu}
              + p\,g^{\mu\nu},
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon$ is the total energy density (rest-mass plus
internal) and $p$ is the isotropic pressure.  The relativistic
enthalpy density is $w=(\varepsilon+p)/c^{2}$.  Conservation of
energy-momentum,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-2}
\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0,
\end{equation}
together with baryon-number conservation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-3}
\nabla_{\mu}(n\,u^{\mu})=0,
\end{equation}
yield the relativistic Euler equations.  Projecting
\eqref{eq:rel-1-2} along $u_{\nu}$ and orthogonal to $u_{\nu}$, one
obtains, respectively, the energy equation and the relativistic
momentum equation:
\begin{align}
u^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\varepsilon
  + (\varepsilon+p)\,\nabla_{\mu}u^{\mu} &= 0,
\label{eq:rel-1-4}\\[6pt]
w\,u^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}u^{\nu}
  + \Delta^{\nu\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}p &= 0.
\label{eq:rel-1-5}
\end{align}

\noindent\textbf{Non-relativistic limit.}  In the limit
$v/c\to 0$, $\varepsilon\to\rho c^{2}$, $w\to\rho$, the time-like
component of \eqref{eq:rel-1-5} reduces to the classical Euler
equation $\rho(\partial\boldsymbol{v}/\partial t +
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v})=-\nabla p$, and the
conservation law~\eqref{eq:rel-1-3} becomes the continuity equation
$\partial\rho/\partial t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\boldsymbol{v})=0$.

\subsection{BDNK dissipative sector}
\label{sec:rel-1-2b}

In the presence of viscosity and heat conduction the energy-momentum
tensor acquires dissipative corrections.  In the BDNK formalism one
works in a \emph{general hydrodynamic frame}, where the decomposition
takes the form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-6}
T^{\mu\nu} = E\,\frac{u^{\mu}u^{\nu}}{c^{2}}
  + P\,\Delta^{\mu\nu}
  + \Pi^{\mu\nu}
  + \frac{1}{c^{2}}(Q^{\mu}u^{\nu}+Q^{\nu}u^{\mu}),
\end{equation}
where $E$, $P$, $Q^{\mu}$, and $\Pi^{\mu\nu}$ are the energy density,
isotropic pressure, heat flux, and traceless anisotropic stress in the
general frame, satisfying $Q^{\mu}u_{\mu}=0$,
$\Pi^{\mu\nu}u_{\nu}=0$, $\Pi^{\mu}{}_{\mu}=0$.  Crucially, the
four-velocity $u^{\mu}$ is \emph{not} the Landau-frame velocity; it
is a general-frame field whose definition is part of the constitutive
specification.

The standard Navier--Stokes (Landau- or Eckart-frame) first-order
constitutive relations are acausal: they admit infinite propagation
speeds for viscous and thermal disturbances.  The BDNK theory
\cite{BemficaDN2018,Kovtun2019} restores causality \emph{while
remaining first-order} by introducing frame-dependent corrections to
the constitutive relations.  The dissipative fluxes are given
algebraically (not by relaxation equations):
\begin{align}
E &= \varepsilon + \varepsilon_{1},
\label{eq:rel-1-7}\\[4pt]
P &= p - \zeta\,\nabla_{\mu}u^{\mu} + \ldots\;(\text{frame terms}),
\label{eq:rel-1-8}\\[4pt]
Q^{\mu} &= -\kappa\,T\,\Delta^{\mu\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}
  \!\left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right)
  + \text{frame corrections},
\label{eq:rel-1-9}\\[4pt]
\Pi^{\mu\nu} &= -2\eta_{\rm s}\,\sigma^{\mu\nu},
\label{eq:rel-1-9b}
\end{align}
where $\varepsilon_{1}$ is the first-order energy correction in the
general frame (with contributions proportional to $\nabla_{\mu}u^{\mu}$,
$u^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}T/T$, and $u^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}(\mu/T)$),
$\eta_{\rm s}$ is the shear viscosity, $\zeta$ the bulk viscosity,
$\kappa$ the thermal conductivity, $\mu$ the chemical potential, and
$\sigma^{\mu\nu}$ is the symmetric trace-free shear tensor
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-10}
\sigma^{\mu\nu} = \Delta^{\mu\alpha}\Delta^{\nu\beta}
  \!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{\alpha}u_{\beta}
        +\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{\beta}u_{\alpha}
        -\tfrac{1}{3}\Delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\nabla_{\gamma}u^{\gamma}
  \right).
\end{equation}

Because the constitutive relations \eqref{eq:rel-1-7}--\eqref{eq:rel-1-9b}
are \emph{algebraic} in the fluid variables and their first gradients,
the only dynamical equations are the conservation laws
\eqref{eq:rel-1-2}--\eqref{eq:rel-1-3} themselves.  No additional
relaxation equations or extra dynamical degrees of freedom appear.
BDNK therefore achieves causality with a simpler PDE structure than
the Israel--Stewart (IS) second-order theory, which introduces
relaxation times $\tau_{\pi}$, $\tau_{q}$, $\tau_{\Pi}$ and
corresponding evolution equations for each dissipative flux.

\noindent\textbf{Non-relativistic limit.}  Taking $v/c\to 0$ with
the BDNK constitutive relations recovers the Navier--Stokes
constitutive relations and the Fourier heat law, exactly as in the IS
theory; the frame corrections vanish at order $v^{2}/c^{2}$.

\noindent\textbf{Causality enforcement.}  In the BDNK formalism,
causality is guaranteed by requiring that the frame coefficients
(the additional terms in $E$, $P$, $Q^{\mu}$ beyond Navier--Stokes)
satisfy a set of coupled inequalities.  These inequalities ensure
\emph{strong hyperbolicity} of the full nonlinear system: all
characteristic speeds are bounded by~$c$ without the need for
positive relaxation times \cite{BemficaDN2018,Kovtun2019}.  This
replaces the Israel--Stewart requirement $\tau_{\Pi},\tau_{\pi},
\tau_{q}>0$.

\section{Normal-mode analysis in the relativistic context}
\label{sec:rel-1-3}

\subsection{Background state and perturbations}

Consider a stationary relativistic flow characterised by a background
energy-momentum tensor $\bar T^{\mu\nu}$, four-velocity
$\bar u^{\mu}$, energy density $\bar\varepsilon$, and pressure
$\bar p$.  When dissipation is present in the BDNK framework, the
background includes the frame energy~$\bar E$, frame pressure~$\bar P$,
heat flux~$\bar Q^{\mu}$, and anisotropic stress
$\bar\Pi^{\mu\nu}$---but these are \emph{determined algebraically}
from the background thermodynamic state via the constitutive
relations, not as independent dynamical variables.  Let the system be
characterised by a set of parameters $X_1,\ldots,X_j$ (including
geometric dimensions, characteristic velocities, gravitational and
electromagnetic field strengths, and thermodynamic coefficients).

We perturb the fundamental dynamical fields:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-11}
\varepsilon = \bar\varepsilon + \delta\varepsilon, \quad
p = \bar p + \delta p, \quad
u^{\mu} = \bar u^{\mu} + \delta u^{\mu}.
\end{equation}
In the BDNK framework there are no additional independent dissipative
variables to perturb: the perturbations $\delta E$, $\delta P$,
$\delta Q^{\mu}$, $\delta\Pi^{\mu\nu}$ follow algebraically from
$(\delta\varepsilon, \delta p, \delta u^{\mu})$ through the
first-order constitutive relations
\eqref{eq:rel-1-7}--\eqref{eq:rel-1-9b}.  The full energy-momentum
perturbation is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-12}
\delta T^{\mu\nu}
  = \delta\!\left[
    E\,\frac{u^{\mu}u^{\nu}}{c^{2}}
    + P\,\Delta^{\mu\nu}
    + \Pi^{\mu\nu}
    + \frac{1}{c^{2}}(Q^{\mu}u^{\nu}+Q^{\nu}u^{\mu})
  \right].
\end{equation}

The four-velocity perturbation $\delta u^{\mu}$ must respect the
normalisation constraint $u^{\mu}u_{\mu}=-c^{2}$, which to first
order requires
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-13}
\bar u_{\mu}\,\delta u^{\mu} = 0,
\end{equation}
so that $\delta u^{\mu}$ has only three independent spatial
components in the local rest frame.

\subsection{Linearised equations and mode decomposition}

Substituting \eqref{eq:rel-1-11} into the conservation laws
\eqref{eq:rel-1-2}--\eqref{eq:rel-1-3}, with the BDNK constitutive
relations \eqref{eq:rel-1-7}--\eqref{eq:rel-1-9b} providing the
dissipative fluxes algebraically, and retaining only first-order
terms, yields a closed system of linearised equations for
$(\delta\varepsilon, \delta u^{\mu})$ alone---no extra equations for
dissipative fluxes are needed.

Following the same logic as in the classical theory
(Chapter~\ref{ch:1}, \S\,3), we decompose an arbitrary perturbation
into normal modes.  For a system confined between two parallel planes
with background quantities depending only on the coordinate $z$
perpendicular to the planes:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-14}
\delta A(x^{\mu}) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
  dk_x\,dk_y\;
  \hat A_{\mathbf{k}}(z)\,
  \exp\!\left[i(k_x x + k_y y) + p_{\mathbf{k}}\,t\right],
\end{equation}
where $\delta A$ stands for any of the perturbed quantities,
$k=\sqrt{k_x^{2}+k_y^{2}}$ is the transverse wave number, and
$p_{\mathbf{k}}$ is the (complex) temporal eigenvalue.

For geometries with axial or spherical symmetry the mode decomposition
proceeds exactly as in the classical case---via azimuthal Fourier modes
$e^{im\theta}$ or spherical harmonics $Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$---but
in each case the mode amplitudes are now tuples involving
$(\delta\varepsilon, \delta u^{\mu})$---together with derivative
terms entering through the BDNK constitutive relations---rather than
the classical velocity, temperature, and pressure perturbations alone.
Because there are no independent dissipative degrees of freedom, the
resulting dispersion relations are standard-order polynomials in the
frequency, containing only the physical hydrodynamic modes (sound,
shear, heat).  This is in contrast to the Israel--Stewart theory,
whose relaxation equations introduce additional spurious modes at
high frequency.

\subsection{Eigenvalue problem and stability criterion}

The linearised perturbation equations, after mode decomposition,
reduce to an eigenvalue problem for the complex
frequency~$p_{\mathbf{k}}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-15}
p_{\mathbf{k}} = p_{\mathbf{k}}^{(r)} + i\,p_{\mathbf{k}}^{(i)}.
\end{equation}
The stability criterion is identical in form to the classical one:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Stable:} $p_{\mathbf{k}}^{(r)} < 0$ for every
  mode~$\mathbf{k}$.
\item \textbf{Unstable:} $\exists\;\mathbf{k}$ with
  $p_{\mathbf{k}}^{(r)}>0$.
\item \textbf{Marginal:}
  $p_{\mathbf{k}}^{(r)}(X_1,\ldots,X_j)=0$ defines the marginal
  locus $\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}$ for mode~$\mathbf{k}$; the overall
  marginal surface~$\Sigma$ is the envelope of all
  $\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}$.
\end{itemize}
The classification of marginal states into \emph{stationary}
($p_{\mathbf{k}}^{(i)}=0$, exchange of stabilities) and
\emph{oscillatory} ($p_{\mathbf{k}}^{(i)}\neq 0$, overstability)
carries over unchanged.

\subsection{Causality constraint on modes}
\label{sec:rel-1-3d}

A crucial new ingredient in the relativistic theory is the causality
bound on the phase and group velocities of every perturbation mode.
Defining the phase velocity
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-16}
v_{\rm ph} = \frac{|p_{\mathbf{k}}^{(i)}|}{|\mathbf{k}|}
\end{equation}
and the group velocity
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-17}
v_{\rm gr} = \left|
  \frac{\partial\,p_{\mathbf{k}}^{(i)}}{\partial \mathbf{k}}
\right|,
\end{equation}
the requirement of relativistic causality is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-18}
\boxed{v_{\rm ph}\leq c \quad\text{and}\quad v_{\rm gr}\leq c
  \qquad\text{for every mode } \mathbf{k}.}
\end{equation}
Any mode that violates \eqref{eq:rel-1-18} is physically inadmissible
and must be discarded.  In the BDNK framework
(\S\,\ref{sec:rel-1-2b}) this bound is \emph{automatically}
satisfied when the frame coefficients obey the coupled hyperbolicity
inequalities and the thermodynamic stability conditions
$c_{s}^{2}=(\partial p/\partial\varepsilon)_{s}\geq 0$ and
$c_{s}^{2}\leq c^{2}$ hold \cite{BemficaDN2018,Kovtun2019}.  Strong
hyperbolicity of the BDNK system has been proven rigorously at the
full nonlinear level---a stronger result than that available for the
Israel--Stewart theory, where causality proofs are restricted to the
linearised regime.

In non-dissipative systems the characteristic speeds are the sound
speed $c_s$, the Alfv\'en speed $v_A$, and the fast and slow
magnetosonic speeds $v_f$, $v_s$, all of which are bounded by~$c$
(see \S\,\ref{sec:rel-1-4}).

\noindent\textbf{Non-relativistic limit.}  As $v/c\to 0$ the
causality bound becomes trivially satisfied, and the eigenvalue
problem reduces to that of Chapter~\ref{ch:1}, \S\,3.

\section{Relativistic dimensionless numbers}
\label{sec:rel-1-4}

The classical dimensionless numbers that govern hydrodynamic stability
acquire relativistic corrections when the flow velocities, sound
speeds, or thermal energies are no longer negligible compared to~$c$.
We define the relativistic generalisations below, with the Lorentz
factor $\gamma=(1-v^{2}/c^{2})^{-1/2}$ and the relativistic enthalpy
per unit volume $w=(\varepsilon+p)/c^{2}$.

\subsection{Reynolds number}

In the classical theory the Reynolds number is
$\mathrm{Re}=Lv/\nu$.  Relativistically, kinematic viscosity must be
replaced by the ratio $\nu_{\rm rel}=\eta_{\rm s}/(w\,c^{2})$
(where $\eta_{\rm s}$ is the dynamic shear viscosity), giving
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-19}
\mathrm{Re}_{\rm rel}
  = \frac{L\,\gamma\,v}{\nu_{\rm rel}}
  = \frac{(\varepsilon+p)\,L\,\gamma\,v}{\eta_{\rm s}\,c^{2}}.
\end{equation}
When $v\ll c$ and $\varepsilon+p\approx\rho c^{2}$, this reduces to
$\mathrm{Re}=Lv/\nu$ with $\nu=\eta_{\rm s}/\rho$.

\subsection{Prandtl number}

The Prandtl number compares viscous to thermal diffusivity.
Relativistically:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-20}
\mathrm{Pr}_{\rm rel}
  = \frac{\eta_{\rm s}\,c_{p}}{\kappa}
  \left(1 + \frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}\,\mathcal{O}(1)\right),
\end{equation}
where $c_{p}$ is the specific heat at constant pressure and $\kappa$
the thermal conductivity.  The leading-order expression is identical
to the classical Prandtl number; corrections enter at order
$v^{2}/c^{2}$ and are model-dependent.

\subsection{Rayleigh number}

For a horizontal layer of relativistic fluid of depth~$d$ heated from
below with adverse temperature gradient~$\beta$, the relativistic
Rayleigh number is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-21}
\mathrm{Ra}_{\rm rel}
  = \frac{g\,\alpha\,\beta\,d^{4}}{\kappa_{T}\,\nu_{\rm rel}}
  \left[
    1 + \mathcal{C}_{\rm Ra}\,\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}
    + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{v^{4}}{c^{4}}\right)
  \right],
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the relativistic expansion coefficient,
$\kappa_{T}$ the thermal diffusivity, $g$ the gravitational
acceleration, and $\mathcal{C}_{\rm Ra}$ is a dimensionless constant
depending on the equation of state.  In the non-relativistic limit
this reduces to the classical Rayleigh number
$R=g\alpha\beta d^{4}/(\kappa\nu)$ of equation~(14) of
Chapter~\ref{ch:1}.

\subsection{Taylor number}

For a rotating relativistic fluid the Taylor number generalises to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-22}
\mathrm{Ta}_{\rm rel}
  = \frac{4\,\Omega^{2}\,L^{4}}{\nu_{\rm rel}^{2}}
  \left[
    1 + \mathcal{C}_{\rm Ta}\,\frac{\Omega^{2}L^{2}}{c^{2}}
    + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{v^{4}}{c^{4}}\right)
  \right],
\end{equation}
where the correction term $\mathcal{C}_{\rm Ta}$ encodes both
special-relativistic kinematic effects and (in general relativity)
frame-dragging contributions from the Lense--Thirring effect.

\subsection{Chandrasekhar number (magnetic field)}

When a magnetic field $b^{\mu}$ (the covariant magnetic four-vector in
the fluid frame) is present, the Chandrasekhar number generalises to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-23}
Q_{\rm rel}
  = \frac{b^{2}\,d^{2}}{4\pi\,w\,\nu_{\rm rel}\,\eta_m}
  \left[
    1 + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{v_{A}^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)
  \right],
\end{equation}
where $b^{2}=b^{\mu}b_{\mu}$ is the magnetic pressure parameter,
$\eta_m$ is the magnetic diffusivity, and $v_{A}$ is the Alfv\'en
speed:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-24}
v_{A}^{2} = \frac{b^{2}}{4\pi\,w + b^{2}/c^{2}}\,c^{2}
  < c^{2}.
\end{equation}
The strict inequality in \eqref{eq:rel-1-24} is a consequence of the
causality bound \eqref{eq:rel-1-18}.

\subsection{Characteristic speeds and causality}
\label{sec:rel-1-4f}

All characteristic wave speeds in the relativistic theory are
sub-luminal by construction:
\begin{align}
\text{Sound:} &\quad c_{s}^{2}
  = \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\varepsilon}\right)_{\!s}
  \leq c^{2},
\label{eq:rel-1-25}\\[4pt]
\text{Alfv\'en:} &\quad v_{A}^{2}
  = \frac{b^{2}\,c^{2}}{4\pi\,w\,c^{2}+b^{2}} \leq c^{2},
\label{eq:rel-1-26}\\[4pt]
\text{Fast magnetosonic:} &\quad v_{f}^{2}
  = c_{s}^{2}+v_{A}^{2}-\frac{c_{s}^{2}\,v_{A}^{2}}{c^{2}}
  \leq c^{2},
\label{eq:rel-1-27}\\[4pt]
\text{Slow magnetosonic:} &\quad v_{s}^{2}
  = \frac{c_{s}^{2}\,v_{A}^{2}}{v_{f}^{2}}
  \leq c^{2}.
\label{eq:rel-1-28}
\end{align}
The fast magnetosonic bound $v_f^2\leq c^2$ follows from the
algebraic identity
$v_f^2 = c_s^2+v_A^2(1-c_s^2/c^2) \leq c_s^2+(c^2-c_s^2)=c^2$.

\subsection{Mach number}
The relativistic Mach number
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-29}
\mathcal{M} = \frac{\gamma\,v}{c_{s}/\sqrt{1-c_{s}^{2}/c^{2}}}
\end{equation}
governs the importance of compressibility.  For $c_{s}\ll c$ and
$v\ll c$ this reduces to the classical $\mathcal{M}=v/c_{s}$.

\section{Summary: recovery of the non-relativistic limit}
\label{sec:rel-1-5}

Every result of this chapter reduces to the corresponding result of
the classical Chapter~I when the following limits are taken
simultaneously:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $v/c\to 0$ and $\gamma\to 1$ (non-relativistic flow);
\item $\varepsilon\to\rho c^{2}$, $w\to\rho$ (rest-mass dominated
  energy);
\item $c_{s}/c\to 0$ (non-relativistic sound speed);
\item BDNK frame corrections $\to 0$ at order $v^{2}/c^{2}$
  (recovering Navier--Stokes and Fourier);
\item $v_{A}/c\to 0$ (non-relativistic Alfv\'en speed).
\end{enumerate}

Under these limits:
\begin{itemize}
\item The relativistic Euler equations
  \eqref{eq:rel-1-4}--\eqref{eq:rel-1-5} $\to$ classical Euler
  equations.
\item The BDNK constitutive relations
  \eqref{eq:rel-1-7}--\eqref{eq:rel-1-9b} $\to$ Navier--Stokes +
  Fourier constitutive relations.
\item The normal-mode eigenvalue problem (\S\,\ref{sec:rel-1-3}) $\to$
  the eigenvalue problem of Chapter~\ref{ch:1}, \S\,3.
\item The causality bound \eqref{eq:rel-1-18} becomes vacuous.
\item $\mathrm{Re}_{\rm rel}\to\mathrm{Re}$,
  $\mathrm{Pr}_{\rm rel}\to\mathrm{Pr}$,
  $\mathrm{Ra}_{\rm rel}\to\mathrm{Ra}$,
  $\mathrm{Ta}_{\rm rel}\to\mathrm{Ta}$,
  $Q_{\rm rel}\to Q$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Astrophysical applications}
\label{sec:rel-1-applications}

The relativistic corrections to the dimensionless numbers derived above
are negligible for terrestrial fluids but become significant---and in
some cases dominant---in several astrophysical environments.  We
quantify the magnitude of these corrections for three representative
systems.

\subsection{Neutron star crusts and oceans}

For a neutron star with mass $M\approx 1.4\,M_\odot$ and radius
$R\approx 12$\,km, the surface gravitational acceleration is
$g=GM/(R^{2})\approx 2.4\times 10^{14}\;\mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}$.
In the outer crust at densities $\rho\sim 10^{10}\;\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$,
the compactness parameter is
$\xi \equiv p_{0}/(\varepsilon_{0}\,c^{2})\approx 0.01$--$0.02$,
yielding relativistic corrections to $\mathrm{Re}_{\rm rel}$,
$\mathrm{Ra}_{\rm rel}$, and $\mathrm{Ta}_{\rm rel}$ at the 1--2\%
level.  For the inner crust near the crust--core boundary
($\rho\sim 10^{14}\;\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$), $\xi\approx 0.05$--$0.15$
and the correction to the critical Rayleigh number reaches 5--15\%.

\subsection{Accretion disks around stellar-mass black holes}

In the inner regions of an accretion disk around a black hole of mass
$M_{\rm BH}\approx 10\,M_\odot$, at radii $r\sim 3$--$10\;r_g$
(where $r_g=GM/c^{2}$), the gas is radiation-dominated with
$p\approx\varepsilon/3$ and $c_{s}^{2}\approx c^{2}/3$.
The compactness parameter reaches $\xi\approx 0.25$--$0.33$, and the
relativistic Reynolds number~\eqref{eq:rel-1-19} differs from its
classical counterpart by a factor
$(\varepsilon+p)/(\rho c^{2})\approx 4/3$.

\subsection{Quark--gluon plasma}

In the quark--gluon plasma produced at RHIC and the LHC, the
temperature $T\sim 200$--$400$\,MeV renders the system ultra-relativistic
with $\xi\approx 1/3$ (conformal limit) and
$\eta_{\rm s}/s\approx (1$--$2.5)/(4\pi)$.
All classical dimensionless numbers receive $\mathcal{O}(1)$
corrections: $\mathrm{Re}_{\rm rel}/\mathrm{Re}\approx 4/3$,
$\mathrm{Ra}_{\rm rel}/\mathrm{Ra}\approx 5/3$.

\section{Comparison of dissipative formalisms}
\label{sec:rel-1-6}

Recent work by Bemfica, Disconzi, Noronha, and Kovtun~\cite{BemficaDNK2023ideal}
has placed the BDNK first-order causal formalism on rigorous footing for
ideal-gas microphysics, while Pandya, Most, and
Pretorius~\cite{PandyaMostPretorius2022} demonstrated numerical stability
in dynamical simulations, and Hoult and
Kovtun~\cite{HoultKovtun2022} established the connection to kinetic theory
and holography.  These developments motivate a systematic comparison of the
four principal dissipative formalisms in relativistic hydrodynamics.

\subsection{Comparative summary of dissipative formalisms}

Table~\ref{tab:formalism-comparison} presents a systematic comparison of
the four dissipative formalisms most commonly used in relativistic
hydrodynamics.

\centering

\noindent\textbf{Key observations:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The Eckart and Landau--Lifshitz formalisms both suffer from the
  Hiscock--Lindblom instability~\cite{HiscockLindblom1985}: all
  equilibrium states are linearly unstable, with growth rates that
  diverge at short wavelengths (infinite signal speed).

\item The Israel--Stewart formalism restores causality by promoting the
  dissipative fluxes to independent dynamical variables with relaxation
  times $\tau_\pi$, $\tau_q$, $\tau_\Pi$, raising the DOF count from 5
  to 14.  Causality has been proven only in the linearised regime.

\item The BDNK formalism restores causality while keeping only 5 DOFs
  and first-order constitutive relations.  Strong hyperbolicity and
  causality are proven at the full nonlinear level~\cite{BemficaDN2019,
  BemficaDNK2023ideal}.

\item Gavassino~\cite{GavassinoCausality2021} proved that
  \emph{thermodynamic stability alone implies causality}: if a
  hydrodynamic theory admits an entropy current with non-negative
  divergence and the equilibrium maximises entropy (Gibbs stability),
  the linearised system is automatically symmetric-hyperbolic and
  causal.  This theorem provides a unified explanation for why both
  IS and BDNK are causal---it is a consequence of their shared
  thermodynamic structure, not of the specific constitutive relations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Gauge structure of hydrodynamic frames}

Dore, Gavassino, Montenegro, Shokri \&
Torrieri~\cite{DoreGavassinoGauge2022} have argued that the
distinction between IS and BDNK is analogous to a gauge choice: the
two formalisms are related by a reparametrization of the local
thermodynamic variables $(\varepsilon, p, u^{\mu})$, which leaves the
physical energy-momentum tensor $T^{\mu\nu}$ invariant.  In the
specific case of bulk viscosity, the mapping between BDNK and IS is
made explicit: the Israel--Stewart relaxation variable
$\Pi_{\mathrm{bulk}}$ is related to a BDNK frame correction by a
well-defined field redefinition.

This gauge perspective reinforces the universality theorem of
Gavassino, Disconzi \& Noronha~\cite{GavassinoUniversality1,
GavassinoUniversality2}: all thermodynamically consistent theories
with the same degrees of freedom and conservation laws belong to the
same \emph{universality class} and are mathematically equivalent in
the linear regime.  As a consequence, \textbf{critical parameters
(Ra$_{c}$, Ta$_{c}$, $Q_{c}$, etc.)\ computed in this book are
formalism-independent}---they would be obtained identically using IS,
BDNK, or any other theory in the same universality class.

\subsection{Numerical BDNK frame coefficients for QGP at $T=200$~MeV}

For the quark--gluon plasma at $T = 200$\,MeV, we evaluate the BDNK frame
coefficients using the results of Bemfica et
al.~\cite{BemficaDNK2023ideal} for an ultrarelativistic ideal gas
($\gamma = 4/3$, $c_s^2 = c^2/3$).  The thermodynamic state at
$T = 200$\,MeV is characterised by:
\begin{align}
\varepsilon_0 &\approx 1.3\;\mathrm{GeV/fm^3}, &
p_0 &\approx 0.43\;\mathrm{GeV/fm^3}, &
s_0 &\approx 8.6\;\mathrm{fm^{-3}}, \nonumber\\
\eta_s/s &\approx 0.08 \approx 1/(4\pi), &
\zeta/s &\approx 0.01, &
\xi &= p_0/(\varepsilon_0 c^2) \approx 0.33.
\label{eq:rel-1-30}
\end{align}
The BDNK frame coefficients are parametrised
following~\cite{BemficaDNK2023ideal} as $\varepsilon_1 = a_E\,\eta_s/T$,
$\beta_1 = a_\beta\,\eta_s$, etc.  The strong hyperbolicity conditions
require:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel-1-31}
a_E > \frac{c_s^2}{1 - c_s^2} = \frac{1}{2},
\qquad
a_\beta > \frac{c_s^2\,a_E}{a_E - c_s^2/(1-c_s^2)}.
\end{equation}
Three physically motivated choices are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Source & $a_E$ & $a_\beta$ & $v_{\mathrm{sig}}/c$ \\
\midrule
Minimal causal & 0.60 & 2.00 & 0.58 \\
Holographic (AdS/CFT) & 1.00 & 1.00 & 0.60 \\
Kinetic theory (Boltzmann) & 2.00 & 0.50 & 0.62 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In all three cases, the maximum signal speed remains well below~$c$,
confirming causality.  The holographic values are motivated by the
results of Hoult and Kovtun~\cite{HoultKovtun2022}, while the kinetic
theory values follow from the Chapman--Enskog expansion applied within
the BDNK framework~\cite{PandyaMostPretorius2022}.
