\chapter{Preface}
\label{ch:preface}

Chandrasekhar's 1961 monograph \emph{Hydrodynamic and Hydromagnetic Stability}
remains one of the most influential works in theoretical fluid dynamics.  It
provides a comprehensive, self-contained treatment of the principal
instabilities that arise in non-relativistic fluids and plasmas---from thermal
convection and rotating flows to the Rayleigh--Taylor and Kelvin--Helmholtz
instabilities, gravitational collapse, and variational energy principles.

The present volume extends every chapter of Chandrasekhar's treatise into the
domain of special- and general-relativistic fluids.  The motivations are both
theoretical and astrophysical.  On the theoretical side, relativistic
corrections to classical instability criteria reveal new physics: enthalpy
replaces rest-mass density as the inertial quantity; pressure contributes to
gravitational attraction; and dissipative processes must be formulated
carefully to preserve causality and thermodynamic stability.  On the
astrophysical side, relativistic instabilities govern the dynamics of
neutron-star mergers, gamma-ray burst jets, accretion flows around black holes,
and the quark--gluon plasma produced in heavy-ion collisions.

\paragraph{Why BDNK?}
The treatment of viscous and thermally conducting fluids in relativity has a
troubled history.  The classical relativistic Navier--Stokes equations
(Eckart, 1940; Landau \& Lifshitz, 1959) are acausal and linearly unstable
about equilibrium---a pathology first identified by Hiscock and Lindblom
(1985).  The Israel--Stewart second-order theory (1979) restores causality by
promoting dissipative fluxes to independent dynamical variables, but at the
cost of introducing additional evolution equations and a large number of
transport coefficients.

The Bemfica--Disconzi--Noronha--Kovtun (BDNK) formalism, developed from 2018
onward, offers a compelling alternative.  It remains a \emph{first-order}
theory---dissipative fluxes are still algebraic functions of the fluid
variables and their first gradients---but achieves causality and stability
through a careful choice of hydrodynamic frame (the ``out-of-equilibrium''
definitions of temperature, chemical potential, and flow velocity).  The
resulting equations are no more complicated than Navier--Stokes, yet they are
well-posed as an initial-value problem in full general relativity.

This combination of simplicity and rigour makes BDNK the natural framework for
extending Chandrasekhar's linear-stability analyses to relativistic regimes.
Every dissipative correction in this book reduces to the classical
Navier--Stokes result in the non-relativistic limit $v/c \to 0$, while
remaining causal at all speeds.

\paragraph{Structure of this book.}
The book is divided into four parts, mirroring the thematic organisation of
Chandrasekhar's original.  Three preliminary framework chapters develop the
relativistic hydrodynamic, magnetohydrodynamic, and thermodynamic machinery
needed throughout.  Part~I treats relativistic thermal instability
(Chapters~I--VI), Part~II addresses the stability of relativistic flows
(Chapters~VII--IX), Part~III considers relativistic superposed fluids
(Chapters~X--XI), and Part~IV covers relativistic jets, gravitational
instability, and general variational principles (Chapters~XII--XIV).  An
epilogue collects the principal results in tabular form and identifies open
problems.

\paragraph{Prerequisites.}
The reader is assumed to be familiar with Chandrasekhar's original treatise (or
an equivalent background in classical hydrodynamic stability theory) and with
the basics of special and general relativity at the level of a standard
graduate text (e.g., Weinberg, Misner--Thorne--Wheeler, or Carroll).

\chapter{Notation and Conventions}
\label{ch:notation}

We collect here the principal notation and sign conventions used throughout
this book.  A summary table is provided at the end for quick reference.

\section*{General Conventions}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Metric signature:} $(-,+,+,+)$.  The Minkowski metric is
  $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$.
\item \textbf{Speed of light:} $c$ is kept explicit in all formulae to
  facilitate taking the non-relativistic limit $v/c \to 0$.
\item \textbf{Units:} Gaussian--CGS units for electromagnetic quantities;
  $G$ and $k_B$ are kept explicit.
\item \textbf{Index conventions:} Greek indices $\mu, \nu, \alpha, \ldots$
  run over $0,1,2,3$.  Latin indices $i, j, k, \ldots$ run over spatial
  components $1,2,3$.  Einstein summation convention is assumed unless stated
  otherwise.
\item \textbf{Covariant derivative:} $\nabla_\mu$ denotes the covariant
  derivative compatible with $g_{\mu\nu}$.
\item \textbf{Lie derivative:} $\mathcal{L}_\xi$ denotes the Lie derivative
  along the vector field $\xi^\mu$.
\end{itemize}
