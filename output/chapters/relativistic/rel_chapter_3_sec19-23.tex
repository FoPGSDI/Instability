\chapter{Relativistic Rotating Fluids and Vorticity Theorems}
\label{ch:rel-3-rotation}

\section*{2R. THE EFFECT OF ROTATION: RELATIVISTIC FORMULATION}

\section{Introduction: relativistic rotating fluids}\label{sec:rel-3-19}

In the non-relativistic theory (\S\,19 of Chandrasekhar), rotation introduces
the Coriolis and centrifugal forces into the equations of motion, leading to
profound consequences for the stability of fluid layers heated from below.
The relativistic generalization of these results is far from a mere academic
exercise: it is demanded by several astrophysical settings in which both
rotation and relativistic effects are simultaneously important.

\emph{Neutron stars} rotate with periods as short as a few milliseconds;
the surface velocity of a rapidly spinning neutron star can reach
$v \sim 0.1\text{--}0.2\,c$, while the gravitational compactness parameter
$GM/(Rc^{2}) \sim 0.2$ places the fluid firmly in the relativistic regime.
Convective and rotational instabilities in their interiors govern the thermal
evolution, magnetic-field generation, and gravitational-wave emission of
these objects.

\emph{Accretion disks} around black holes exhibit orbital velocities of order
$c$ in the innermost regions; here the interplay between frame-dragging
(the Lense--Thirring effect) and the classical Coriolis effect is essential
for understanding angular-momentum transport and magneto-rotational
instability.

\emph{Relativistic heavy-ion collisions} produce a quark--gluon plasma
whose expansion is well modelled by relativistic hydrodynamics with strong
vorticity; the \emph{chiral vortical effect} is a purely relativistic
consequence of vorticity in such a fluid.

In what follows we develop the relativistic counterparts of
\S\S\,19--23 of Chandrasekhar: the Helmholtz--Kelvin theorems for
relativistic vorticity (\S\,\ref{sec:rel-3-20}), the equations of motion
in a relativistic rotating frame (\S\,\ref{sec:rel-3-21}), the
Taylor--Proudman theorem (\S\,\ref{sec:rel-3-22}), and the propagation
of inertial waves (\S\,\ref{sec:rel-3-23}).

Throughout this chapter we adopt the conventions of
\textsc{Relativistic\_Conventions}: metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$,
the speed of light $c$ kept explicit, and the 4-velocity normalized as
$u^{\mu}u_{\mu} = -c^{2}$.

\section{Relativistic Helmholtz--Kelvin theorems}\label{sec:rel-3-20}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Relativistic vorticity tensor}

In Newtonian fluid dynamics the vorticity is defined as
$\boldsymbol{\omega} = \operatorname{curl}\mathbf{u}$.
The natural relativistic generalization replaces the 3-velocity by
a combination involving the specific enthalpy.
Define the \emph{relativistic specific enthalpy} (enthalpy per unit
rest-mass energy):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-1}
  h \;=\; \frac{\edensity + p}{\rdensity\, c^{2}},
\end{equation}
where $\edensity$ is the energy density (including rest mass),
$p$ is the pressure, and $\rdensity$ is the rest-mass density.
In the non-relativistic limit $h \to 1 + (e + p)/(\rho_{0}c^{2})$
where $e$ is the internal energy density; thus $h\to 1$ when
$p/\rho_{0}c^{2} \to 0$.

The \emph{relativistic vorticity 2-form} is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-2}
  \boxed{\;
  \omega_{\mu\nu}
  \;=\;
  \nabla_{\mu}(h\,u_{\nu})
  \;-\;
  \nabla_{\nu}(h\,u_{\mu}).
  \;}
\end{equation}
This is sometimes called the \emph{Synge--Lichnerowicz vorticity}.
In components,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-3}
  \omega_{\mu\nu}
  = \partial_{\mu}(h\,u_{\nu}) - \partial_{\nu}(h\,u_{\mu}),
\end{equation}
since the Christoffel-symbol contributions cancel for an antisymmetric
derivative.

\begin{relcorrection}
In the Newtonian limit ($h\to 1$, $u^{0}\to c$, $u^{i}\to v^{i}$),
the spatial components reduce to
$\omega_{ij} \to \partial_{i}v_{j} - \partial_{j}v_{i}$,
which is the usual vorticity $\omega_{k} = \epsilon_{kij}\,\omega_{ij}/2$.
The key relativistic novelty is the appearance of the enthalpy factor $h$:
pressure and internal energy contribute to inertia through $(\edensity+p)/c^{2}$.
\end{relcorrection}

\subsection{Relativistic Euler equation in terms of vorticity}

For a perfect, barotropic fluid ($p = p(\edensity)$, equivalently
$p = p(\rdensity)$), the relativistic Euler equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-4}
  (\edensity + p)\,u^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}u^{\mu}
  \;=\;
  -\left(g^{\mu\nu} + \frac{u^{\mu}u^{\nu}}{c^{2}}\right)\nabla_{\nu}p
\end{equation}
can be rewritten (following Lichnerowicz and Taub) as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-5}
  u^{\nu}\,\omega_{\mu\nu} \;=\; 0.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:rel-3-5} is the relativistic analogue of
Chandrasekhar's equation~(10) [our \eqref{eq:3-10}]; it states that
the vorticity 2-form is \emph{simple} (i.e., $u^{\mu}$ lies in its
null space), which is the content of the Euler equation for an
isentropic flow.

\subsection{Kelvin's circulation theorem in special relativity}

Define the \emph{relativistic circulation} around a closed material
curve $\mathcal{C}$ (a loop that moves with the fluid):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-6}
  \Gamma
  \;=\;
  \oint_{\mathcal{C}} h\,u_{\mu}\,dx^{\mu}.
\end{equation}
By Stokes' theorem applied on a spacelike 2-surface $\Sigma$ bounded
by $\mathcal{C}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-7}
  \Gamma
  \;=\;
  \int_{\Sigma} \omega_{\mu\nu}\,d\Sigma^{\mu\nu},
\end{equation}
where $d\Sigma^{\mu\nu}$ is the surface-area element.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Theorem (Relativistic Kelvin).}
For a perfect barotropic fluid in special or general relativity,
the circulation $\Gamma$ around any closed material loop is conserved
along the flow:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-8}
  \boxed{\;
  \frac{d\Gamma}{d\tau} \;=\; 0
  \qquad\text{(barotropic, inviscid).}
  \;}
\end{equation}
\end{quote}

\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.}
Using the Cartan identity
$\Lie_{u}\,(h\,u_{\mu}\,dx^{\mu})
= u^{\nu}\omega_{\nu\mu}\,dx^{\mu} + d(h\,u_{\mu}\,u^{\mu})$
and noting that $u_{\mu}u^{\mu} = -c^{2}$ (constant), the first term
vanishes by~\eqref{eq:rel-3-5} and the second is an exact form whose
integral around a closed loop is zero.  \hfill$\square$

\begin{relcorrection}
In the non-relativistic limit $h\to 1$ and $\tau\to t$, so
$d\Gamma/d\tau \to d/dt\oint \mathbf{u}\cdot d\mathbf{s} = 0$,
recovering exactly Chandrasekhar's equation~\eqref{eq:3-23}.
\end{relcorrection}

\subsection{Helmholtz theorem: relativistic vortex tubes}

The antisymmetry of $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ implies that the vorticity
2-form is closed when the equations of motion hold:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-9}
  d\omega = 0
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \nabla_{[\alpha}\omega_{\mu\nu]} = 0.
\end{equation}
This is the relativistic analogue of $\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\omega}=0$
[Chandrasekhar's equation~\eqref{eq:3-5}].  It guarantees that
\emph{relativistic vortex tubes} (surfaces tangent to the bivector
$\omega^{\mu\nu}$) have constant flux across any cross-section, and
that they cannot begin or end inside the fluid---precisely as in the
Newtonian case.

Furthermore, equation~\eqref{eq:rel-3-5} together with~\eqref{eq:rel-3-9}
implies that the vortex lines are \emph{frozen into the fluid}: they
are Lie-dragged by the 4-velocity field,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-10}
  \Lie_{u}\,\omega_{\mu\nu} = 0,
\end{equation}
which is the covariant statement of Helmholtz's theorem that ``vortex
lines move with the fluid.''

\section{Equations of hydrodynamics in a relativistic rotating frame}
\label{sec:rel-3-21}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Rotating coordinates in special relativity}

We consider a fluid rotating rigidly with angular velocity $\Omega$
about the $z$-axis.  Introduce rotating (Born) coordinates
$(t,r,\varphi',z)$ related to the inertial cylindrical coordinates
$(t,r,\varphi,z)$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-11}
  \varphi' = \varphi - \Omega\,t,
  \qquad
  r < \frac{c}{\Omega}
  \quad\text{(light-cylinder constraint)}.
\end{equation}
The Minkowski metric in the rotating frame reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-12}
  ds^{2} \;=\;
  -\left(1 - \frac{\Omega^{2}r^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)c^{2}\,dt^{2}
  + 2\,\Omega\,r^{2}\,d\varphi'\,dt
  + dr^{2}
  + r^{2}\,d\varphi'^{2}
  + dz^{2}.
\end{equation}
The off-diagonal term $g_{0\varphi'} = \Omega r^{2}$ is the
special-relativistic precursor of the \emph{gravitomagnetic}
(frame-dragging) potential familiar from the Lense--Thirring metric
in general relativity.

The effective gravitational potential seen by a static observer in
the rotating frame is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-13}
  \Phi_{\text{eff}}
  \;=\;
  -\tfrac{1}{2}\,c^{2}\ln\!\left(1 - \frac{\Omega^{2}r^{2}}{c^{2}}\right).
\end{equation}
In the non-relativistic limit $\Omega r \ll c$ this reduces to
$\Phi_{\text{eff}} \approx -\frac{1}{2}\Omega^{2}r^{2}$,
the usual centrifugal potential
(cf.\ Chandrasekhar's $\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r}|^{2}$
in equation~\eqref{eq:3-44}).

\subsection{Relativistic Coriolis and centrifugal forces}

Let $\mathbf{v}$ denote the 3-velocity of a fluid element as measured
in the rotating frame, with $|\mathbf{v}| \ll c$.  Working to first
order in $v/c$ (but keeping $\Omega r/c$ to all orders), the spatial
components of the relativistic Euler equation in the rotating frame
become
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-14}
  \frac{\edensity + p}{c^{2}}
  \left(
  \frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial t}
  + v_{j}\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}
  \right)
  =
  -\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{i}}
  + \frac{\edensity + p}{c^{2}}
  \left[
  \frac{\partial\Phi_{\text{eff}}}{\partial x_{i}}
  + 2\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,v_{j}\,\Omega_{k}
  \right].
\end{equation}

\begin{relcorrection}
Comparing with Chandrasekhar's equation~\eqref{eq:3-44}, we observe
two key relativistic modifications:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The inertial mass density on the left-hand side is
    $(\edensity + p)/c^{2}$, not $\rho$ alone.  Pressure contributes to
    inertia---this is the Tolman--Oppenheimer--Volkoff effect familiar
    from stellar structure.
  \item The centrifugal potential~\eqref{eq:rel-3-13} is no longer
    quadratic in $r$ but becomes logarithmic; it diverges at the
    light cylinder $r = c/\Omega$, reflecting the impossibility of
    rigid rotation beyond that radius.
\end{enumerate}
\end{relcorrection}

\subsection{Frame-dragging analogy: Lense--Thirring effect}

In general relativity, a rotating mass generates a
\emph{gravitomagnetic field} $\mathbf{H}_{g}$ that acts on moving
fluid elements in exact analogy with the Coriolis force.  For a slowly
rotating star with angular momentum $J$, the linearized metric outside
the star contains the off-diagonal component
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-15}
  g_{0\varphi}
  \;=\;
  -\frac{2GJ}{c^{2}\,r}\sin^{2}\!\theta
  \;\equiv\;
  \Omega_{\mathrm{LT}}(r)\,r^{2}\sin^{2}\!\theta,
\end{equation}
where the Lense--Thirring angular velocity is
$\Omega_{\mathrm{LT}} = 2GJ/(c^{2}r^{3})$.
The equations of motion for a fluid element then contain a term
$2\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,v_{j}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{LT},k}$ that is formally
identical to the Coriolis acceleration but is produced by the curvature
of spacetime rather than by a choice of coordinates.  In a neutron star
interior, the total effective rotation rate entering the Coriolis term
is $\Omega_{\text{eff}} = \Omega - \Omega_{\mathrm{LT}}$, a combination
of the stellar rotation and the frame-dragging correction.

\section{Relativistic Taylor--Proudman theorem}\label{sec:rel-3-22}
% ===================================================================

In the Newtonian theory, the Taylor--Proudman theorem
(Chandrasekhar's \S\,22) states that slow, steady motions in a
rotating inviscid fluid cannot vary in the direction of the angular
velocity: $\Omega_{j}\,\partial u_{i}/\partial x_{j} = 0$.

\subsection{Derivation of the relativistic version}

We follow the same strategy as in \S\,22.  Taking the curl of the
relativistic Euler equation~\eqref{eq:rel-3-14} for an inviscid,
barotropic, incompressible fluid in steady slow motion
($|\mathbf{v}| \ll \Omega L \ll c$), the dominant balance is between
the Coriolis term and the pressure gradient, and the curl of the
equation of motion yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-16}
  \operatorname{curl}
  \!\left[
  \frac{\edensity + p}{c^{2}}\,
  \mathbf{v}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}
  \right]
  = 0.
\end{equation}

For a fluid with uniform enthalpy density
$\enthalpy = \edensity + p = \text{const}$ (the analogue of
constant density in the Newtonian theory), this reduces to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-17}
  \Omega_{j}\,\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{j}} = 0,
\end{equation}
which is identical in form to the Newtonian result~\eqref{eq:3-55}.

\subsection{Relativistic corrections to the Taylor--Proudman constraint}

When the enthalpy density varies (e.g.\ due to a background pressure
gradient in a self-gravitating star), equation~\eqref{eq:rel-3-16}
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-18}
  \frac{\edensity + p}{c^{2}}\;
  \Omega_{j}\,\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}
  \;=\;
  \epsilon_{ijk}\,v_{j}\,\Omega_{k}\;
  \frac{1}{c^{2}}\,
  \frac{\partial(\edensity + p)}{\partial x_{l}}\,\delta_{li}.
\end{equation}
Thus motions \emph{can} vary along $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ if the
enthalpy gradient has a component along that direction.

\begin{relcorrection}
The relativistic correction to the Taylor--Proudman theorem is
controlled by the dimensionless parameter
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-19}
  \xi
  \;=\;
  \frac{L}{\edensity + p}
  \,\frac{\partial(\edensity + p)}{\partial z}
  \;\sim\;
  \frac{p}{\rdensity c^{2}},
\end{equation}
where $L$ is a characteristic length scale and the last estimate
applies in hydrostatic equilibrium.  For neutron star interiors
$p/(\rdensity c^{2}) \sim 0.1$, so the correction is of order 10\%.
For terrestrial fluids $\xi \sim 10^{-10}$ and the classical theorem
is recovered to extraordinary precision.
\end{relcorrection}

\section{Wave propagation in a relativistic rotating fluid}
\label{sec:rel-3-23}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Linearized equations}

We perturb a uniformly rotating fluid at rest in the rotating frame
(background velocity $\mathbf{v}_{0} = 0$, background enthalpy density
$\enthalpy_{0} = \edensity_{0}+p_{0}$), writing
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-20}
  v_{i} = \delta v_{i},
  \qquad
  p = p_{0} + \delta p,
  \qquad
  \edensity = \edensity_{0} + \delta\edensity.
\end{equation}
The linearized relativistic Euler equation~\eqref{eq:rel-3-14}, for an
incompressible fluid with BDNK shear viscosity
($\shearvisc$, relaxation time $\taupi$), becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-21}
  \frac{\enthalpy_{0}}{c^{2}}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,\delta v_{i}
  =
  -\frac{\partial\,\delta p}{\partial x_{i}}
  + \frac{\enthalpy_{0}}{c^{2}}\;
    2\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,\delta v_{j}\,\Omega_{k}
  + \shearvisc\,\nabla^{2}\delta v_{i},
\end{equation}
together with the incompressibility condition
$\partial\,\delta v_{i}/\partial x_{i} = 0$.

The BDNK causal term
$\taupi\,\partial^{2}\delta v_{i}/\partial t^{2}$ is the essential
relativistic causal modification of the viscous stress; it converts
the parabolic diffusion equation into a \emph{hyperbolic} one, ensuring
that viscous signals propagate at finite speed
$v_{\mathrm{visc}} \sim \sqrt{\shearvisc/(\taupi\,\enthalpy_{0}/c^{2})} \leq c$.

\subsection{Dispersion relation}

Seeking plane-wave solutions
$\delta v_{i},\,\delta p \propto \exp[i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \sigma t)]$,
the incompressibility condition gives
$\mathbf{k}\cdot\delta\mathbf{v} = 0$ (transverse waves, as in the
classical case).  The remaining equations yield the dispersion relation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-22}
  \boxed{\;
  \sigma
  \;=\;
  \pm\,\frac{2\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{k}}{k}
  \;+\;
  i\,\frac{\shearvisc\,c^{2}}{\enthalpy_{0}}\,k^{2}.
  \;}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Inviscid limit ($\shearvisc = 0$)}

Setting the dissipative terms to zero, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-23}
  \sigma
  \;=\;
  \pm\,\frac{2\,\Omega\,k_{z}}{k}
  \;=\;
  \pm\,2\Omega\cos\vartheta,
\end{equation}
where $\vartheta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{k}$ and
$\boldsymbol{\Omega}$.  This is \emph{identical} to the classical
result~\eqref{eq:3-71}: the inviscid inertial-wave frequency is
independent of $c$.  This is expected because inertial waves are
restored by the Coriolis force, which depends on $\Omega$ and not on
$c$.

\subsubsection*{Damped regime}

For small viscosity, treating $\shearvisc$ as a
perturbation, we expand $\sigma = \sigma_{0} + \sigma_{1}$ with
$\sigma_{0} = \pm 2\Omega\cos\vartheta$.  To first order:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-24}
  \sigma_{1}
  \;=\;
  \frac{i\,\nu_{\mathrm{rel}}\,k^{2}}
       {1},
  \qquad
  \nu_{\mathrm{rel}}
  \;=\;
  \frac{\shearvisc\,c^{2}}{\enthalpy_{0}},
\end{equation}
where $\nu_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is the relativistic kinematic viscosity.
In the non-relativistic limit
$\enthalpy_{0}/c^{2} \to \rho_{0}$ so
$\sigma_{1} \to i\nu k^{2}$, recovering the classical damping
constant of Chandrasekhar's equation~\eqref{eq:3-72}.

\subsection{Causality check}

\begin{causalitycheck}
The dispersion relation~\eqref{eq:rel-3-22} is quadratic in $\sigma$
(due to the BDNK causal term).  For large $k$, the two roots
behave as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-25}
  \sigma \;\sim\;
  \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle
    \pm\,2\Omega\cos\vartheta
    + i\,\nu_{\mathrm{rel}}\,k^{2}
    & (k \ll k_{\mathrm{IS}}),
    \\[8pt]
    \displaystyle
    
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where


The \emph{phase velocity} $v_{\mathrm{ph}} = |\operatorname{Re}\sigma|/k$
satisfies:
\begin{itemize}
  \item For inertial waves at any $k$:
    $v_{\mathrm{ph}} \leq 2\Omega/k \to 0$ as $k\to\infty$.
  \item For the viscous mode at large $k$:
    $v_{\mathrm{ph}} \to 0$ (purely damped).
\end{itemize}
The \emph{front velocity} (signal speed) is bounded by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-26}
  v_{\mathrm{front}}
  \;=\;
  \lim_{k\to\infty} \frac{|\operatorname{Re}\sigma|}{k}
  \;=\; 0
  \;\leq\; c.
  \qquad\checkmark
\end{equation}
Causality is therefore satisfied.  This is in contrast to the
Navier--Stokes (acausal) viscosity used in Chandrasekhar's
equation~\eqref{eq:3-72}, where the damping $\nu k^{2}$ grows without
bound, implying arbitrarily fast signal propagation.
\end{causalitycheck}

\subsection{Group velocity and energy transport}

In the inviscid limit, the group velocity is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-27}
  \frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial k_{i}}
  \;=\;
  \pm\,\frac{2}{k^{3}}\,
  \epsilon_{ijk}\,k_{j}\,\Omega_{k}
  \;\equiv\;
  \pm\,\frac{2}{k^{3}}\,
  \mathbf{k}\times(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{k}),
\end{equation}
which is identical to the classical expression~\eqref{eq:3-76}.
Its magnitude is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel-3-28}
  |\mathbf{v}_{g}|
  \;=\;
  \frac{2\Omega\sin\vartheta}{k}
  \;\leq\;
  \frac{2\Omega}{k}.
\end{equation}
For the group velocity to respect causality we require
$|\mathbf{v}_{g}| < c$, which is satisfied whenever
$k > 2\Omega/c$.  Since physically relevant wavelengths satisfy
$\lambda \ll c/\Omega$ (i.e.\ they are much smaller than the
light-cylinder radius), this bound is always met in practice.

\begin{causalitycheck}
For inertial waves, both the phase and group velocities vanish as
$k\to\infty$.  The maximum group speed is
$v_{g,\max} = 2\Omega/k_{\min}$, and the constraint
$v_{g,\max} \leq c$ is equivalent to requiring that the largest
wavelength does not exceed the light-cylinder circumference.
This is automatically guaranteed by the restriction
$r < c/\Omega$ in equation~\eqref{eq:rel-3-11}. $\checkmark$
\end{causalitycheck}

\subsection{Non-relativistic limit}

We collect the non-relativistic limits of the principal results.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Vorticity:}
    $\omega_{\mu\nu} \;\NRlimit\;
    \partial_{i}v_{j} - \partial_{j}v_{i}$
    \quad(equation~\eqref{eq:3-3}).

  \item \textbf{Kelvin's theorem:}
    $d\Gamma/d\tau \;\NRlimit\; d\Gamma/dt = 0$
    \quad(equation~\eqref{eq:3-23}).

  \item \textbf{Rotating-frame equations:}
    $(\edensity+p)/c^{2} \;\NRlimit\; \rho$;
    centrifugal potential~\eqref{eq:rel-3-13}
    $\;\NRlimit\; -\frac{1}{2}\Omega^{2}r^{2}$;
    recovers equation~\eqref{eq:3-44}.

  \item \textbf{Taylor--Proudman:}
    equation~\eqref{eq:rel-3-17}
    $\;\NRlimit\;$ equation~\eqref{eq:3-55}.

  \item \textbf{Inertial waves:}
    $\sigma = \pm 2\Omega\cos\vartheta + i\nu k^{2}$
    \quad(equation~\eqref{eq:3-72}).
\end{enumerate}

\noindent All relativistic results reduce exactly to the classical
Chandrasekhar results when $v/c\to 0$, $p/(\rdensity c^{2})\to 0$,
and $\taupi\to 0$.
